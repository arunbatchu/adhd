    Applied Quiz: Family Life And Communication¶

    A second set of questions on this chapter, weighted toward applying the ideas rather than recalling them. Most put you in a household moment and ask which move the chapter endorses.

    1. A mother installs a tracking app on her adult daughter's phone "to help." Her daughter is upset. What test does the chapter apply?¶

    1. Whether the app is genuinely useful for the problem
    2. Who asked — support is what the person asked for; parenting is what you decided they need
    3. Whether the daughter was told about it beforehand
    4. Whether the daughter is financially independent
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. That distinction is the one the chapter says deserves to be printed and taped to a refrigerator, and it notes the same act can be either: setting up a shared calendar with your 24-year-old because she asked is support, while installing a tracking app is parenting. Sending the article she requested is support; sending the fourth unrequested article this week is parenting with citations. Option C misses the point — announcing an uninvited intervention doesn't convert it into an invited one.

    2. A father notices his adult son's car registration has expired. What does ask-first help look like?¶

    1. Renew it himself as a favor and mention it afterward
    2. Say nothing, since noticing without acting is the whole discipline
    3. Ask: "want a hand, want an alarm, or is it handled?" — with is it handled as a genuine option
    4. Set a recurring reminder on his son's behalf
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The protocol has three moves: ask what kind of help is wanted before providing any, wait to be invited into a problem before working on it, and let a decline stand. The chapter stresses that noticing is free but acting on what you noticed requires an invitation — and that "is it handled" must be a real option, because sometimes it is. It also offers a menu worth saying aloud: solutions, company, a reminder system, or just to vent.

    3. Parents of a newly diagnosed 31-year-old feel guilty for the missed years and want to help intensively now. What does the chapter say repairs the past?¶

    1. Helping more now, which compensates for the years of missed support
    2. Taking over the logistics so the adult child can focus on treatment
    3. Funding treatment as a concrete form of amends
    4. Saying once, sincerely, "we were grading you on hardware you didn't have" — then treating them as the competent adult they are
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter is direct that helping more now doesn't repair having missed the diagnosis then, and that the intensive-help impulse arrives from the adult child's side as a second adolescence imposed at the worst possible moment. The reframing sentence comes from Chapter 4 and is delivered once — not repeatedly, and not as a preamble to taking over.

    4. A parent intercepts every impending consequence in their adult child's life. What are the two costs?¶

    1. It prevents calibration, since consequences are where the reward system learns — and it broadcasts "I don't believe you can run your life," landing on the shame file
    2. It is unsustainable for the parent and inconvenient for the child
    3. It creates financial dependence and delays independent living
    4. There are no real costs when the intention is loving
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter calls letting a decline stand the hardest discipline in the chapter, and is explicit that adults own their consequences. A parent who intercepts every falling plate isn't preventing failure — they're preventing calibration — and the message lands on Chapter 11's shame file no matter how lovingly it's meant. Option D is the assumption the whole section is written to dismantle.

    See: Chapter 14

    5. Over years, one parent has become the household's logistics department and now says "I'm the only reason this family functions." What happened?¶

    1. An accurate assessment that should be acknowledged by the family
    2. A failure of the person with ADHD to hold up their end
    3. Caregiver burnout, which is a separate clinical condition
    4. Drift toward management — help became ownership became identity, each step individually reasonable
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter names this the role's central occupational hazard and traces the exact sequence: "I'll just set up the appointment" becomes "I handle all the appointments" becomes the identity claim. The sum is a case manager married to, or parenting, a client. It also holds two truths together — the work is real and pretending otherwise burns people out, and the role is neither sainthood nor victimhood, since a narrative casting the person with ADHD as a burden damages both people.

    6. A couple wants to divide chores fairly. What model does the chapter recommend, and at what price?¶

    1. Strict 50/50 by volume, tracked to keep it honest
    2. Rotating all tasks so neither person gets stuck with the worst ones
    3. Divide by task shape rather than quantity — at the price of renegotiating the fitted split as fair, and armoring the recurring tasks with systems
    4. Assign all recurring tasks to the partner without ADHD
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The standard 50/50-by-volume model fails; the fit model works. A partner with ADHD may be catastrophic at recurring-invisible-deadline tasks like bills and renewals — pure prospective memory — and genuinely excellent at burst tasks like the deep-clean before guests or the crisis repair. The price must be said out loud: burst work counts, visible-when-done work counts, and "I'll just remember" is not a household plan. The chapter also suggests some chores be divided to no one by automating them out of the marriage.

    7. A demand lands at a bad moment, the response disappoints, the family member escalates, an RSD spike follows, and both retreat with their stories confirmed. What does the chapter say about exits?¶

    1. The loop must be interrupted by the person with ADHD, since the trigger originates with them
    2. The loop can be cut at any point by either person — including by calling the loop itself, which is the most advanced move in the chapter
    3. The loop can only be broken with professional help
    4. The loop should be allowed to complete so both sides feel heard
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Two structural facts give the exits: the loop runs on predictable fuel — bad timing, unrequested reminders, character language — and either person can cut it. The family member can change the demand's timing and form, the person with ADHD can deploy Chapter 11's pause, and either can say "we're doing the thing — can we restart this conversation after dinner?" The chapter is clear that calling the loop is not surrender.

    8. After a blowup, a couple apologizes, hugs, and moves on without changing anything. What does the chapter predict?¶

    1. The repair is complete, since the emotional rupture was addressed
    2. The apology should have been more detailed to be effective
    3. A repair that ends in hugs but no redesign schedules the same fight for next month
    4. The couple should avoid the topic entirely in future
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The full repair sequence is: wait out the squall while respecting the other person's longer timeline, repair the relationship before relitigating the content, own your side in mechanism terms without hiding behind them, and close the loop with a system change — what alarm, what board, what timing rule would make this collision less likely? The chapter's summary is that families who repair well don't fight less at first, they fight cheaper, and then actually less.

    9. A family meeting is used to review someone's failures from the past week. What does the chapter predict?¶

    1. It will be uncomfortable but ultimately productive
    2. The first time it's used to prosecute someone's week is the last week they honestly attend — the meeting audits systems, never people
    3. It will work if the criticism is balanced with praise
    4. Weekly meetings are unsuitable for ADHD households generally
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The working agenda runs wins first, then the week ahead, then exactly one system patch, then anything parked during the week — with the mechanics that it ends on time and actions leave in the capture system rather than in heads. The chapter also names what half the value is: abolishing "while I have you...", which it calls the meeting's natural predator, by giving every "we should talk about" a place to wait that isn't 9:30 pm.

    10. A couple fights repeatedly over "I told you about this weeks ago" versus "this is the first I'm hearing of it." What does the chapter say, and what fixes it?¶

    1. Both are usually telling the truth — it was said and never encoded — and a shared calendar replaces the argument with a single memory that fires
    2. One person is misremembering, and a written log will establish who
    3. The couple should agree to repeat all important information twice
    4. The issue is attentiveness and should be addressed in counseling
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Per Chapter 2, both people are usually telling the truth, which is why the fight is unwinnable on its own terms. Shared family calendars are the single highest-value piece of shared infrastructure the book recommends: every commitment in one place both readers can see, every entry alerted, added at the moment of commitment by whoever made it. As the chapter puts it, that's the whole chapter in one object — the load moved off the people and onto the system, so the people can go back to being family.
