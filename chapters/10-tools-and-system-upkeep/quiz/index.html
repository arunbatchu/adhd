    Quiz: Tools And Keeping Systems Alive¶

    Test your understanding of the ADHD toolkit and the skill of keeping systems alive after the novelty wears off with these review questions.

    1. Why does this chapter consider digital calendars the highest-value digital tool for ADHD?¶

    1. They require no setup at all
    2. They are the only tool proven to work for every person with ADHD
    3. They fire and interrupt on schedule, unlike a paper calendar that must be remembered to be checked
    4. They automatically complete tasks on your behalf
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter's mechanical reason is that digital calendars fire and interrupt on schedule, while a paper calendar depends on being remembered to be checked — exactly the prospective memory failure point this book keeps returning to. Option A overstates ease of use as the reason. Option B claims a universality the chapter never asserts. Option D wildly overstates what a calendar does.

    2. What is deadline scaffolding?¶

    1. Splitting a project into staged mini-deadlines, each attached to a witness or consequence, since distant deadlines are motivationally invisible
    2. Waiting until the final deadline and working through the night
    3. A type of physical furniture used to organize paperwork
    4. Asking a supervisor to permanently remove all deadlines
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Deadline scaffolding manufactures near deadlines out of a distant one by splitting a project into staged mini-deadlines, each needing a witness or consequence to actually function. Option B describes the exact pattern scaffolding is designed to prevent. Option C is a literal misreading of "scaffolding." Option D contradicts the chapter's point that deadlines are manufactured, not eliminated.

    3. According to this chapter's discussion of system maintenance, what happens to every organizational system over time?¶

    1. A well-designed system, once built correctly, never needs any further attention
    2. Systems only fail for people who don't truly want to be organized
    3. Only digital systems decay; paper-based systems last indefinitely
    4. Every system will eventually stop working as novelty fuel burns down, and this is normal operation, not failure
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter's central claim about maintenance is that every system eventually stops working as novelty burns down, and that this is normal operation requiring a maintenance loop, not evidence of failure. Option A contradicts the chapter's entire argument for ongoing maintenance. Option B revives a character-based explanation the chapter rejects. Option C is false; the chapter notes even shared calendars and paper planners decay without upkeep.

    4. Why does this chapter recommend "selective visibility" rather than standard minimalism advice when decluttering for ADHD?¶

    1. Because clutter has no real effect on attention or cognitive load
    2. Because minimalism is only appropriate for people without ADHD
    3. Because aggressive tidying can effectively delete working systems — items "put away nicely" become functionally invisible and forgotten — so active surfaces should be cleared while action items stay visible on purpose
    4. Because every object in a room should always remain visible at all times
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter explains that out-of-sight is genuinely out-of-mind for this brain, so tidying away action items can delete a working system — the fix is clearing active surfaces while deliberately keeping action items visible. Option A contradicts the chapter's explicit mechanism linking clutter to attention and load. Option B is an unsupported blanket claim. Option D overcorrects into permanent visibility for everything, which the chapter doesn't recommend for storage items.

    Concept Tested: Decluttering For ADHD

    See: Chapter 10

    5. Why does this chapter recommend layering multiple distraction defenses (distance, friction, silence, urgency) rather than relying on one?¶

    1. Because each single defense tends to fail independently at different moments, while a stack of layers degrades gracefully, since one layer usually catches what another misses
    2. Because a single defense is illegal to use alone
    3. Because using more than one defense doubles a person's willpower
    4. Because layering defenses eliminates all distraction completely with no failures
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter's Omar example shows that any single layer fails weekly, but the stack fails rarely, because when one layer fails another usually still catches the drift. Option B is a fabricated claim. Option C misattributes the mechanism to willpower, which the chapter treats as an unreliable strategy. Option D overstates the result; the chapter is explicit that defenses reduce, never eliminate, distraction.

    6. What does "momentum and activation" mean in this chapter, and how does the five-minute contract use it?¶

    1. Starting and continuing a task cost the same amount of effort, so the five-minute contract makes no real difference
    2. For ADHD, starting is the expensive part while continuing is nearly free once moving, so committing to just five minutes with permission to quit lowers the barrier enough to begin
    3. Momentum only applies to physical exercise, not cognitive tasks
    4. The five-minute contract is a trick that never actually leads to longer work sessions
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter explains that starting is the expensive part of any task for ADHD, while continuing is nearly free once moving — the five-minute contract exploits this by making the barrier small enough to cross, with permission to quit honored on the times it's invoked. Option A denies the asymmetry the chapter's whole argument rests on. Option C narrows the concept to an unrelated domain. Option D contradicts the chapter's observation that most sessions continue past five minutes.

    7. Someone has an overwhelming everything-list and doesn't know what to prioritize this morning. Using this chapter's one-question shortcut, what should they ask?¶

    1. What would look most impressive to complete first?
    2. What is the most enjoyable item on the list?
    3. What has been on the list the longest?
    4. What, left undone, causes the most damage by Friday?
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter's shortcut for overwhelmed mornings is exactly this question — what, left undone, causes the most damage by Friday — with everything else treated as genuinely negotiable. Option A substitutes appearance for actual consequence. Option B lets interest override importance, which the chapter warns distorts ADHD prioritization. Option C uses list tenure as a stand-in for actual stakes, which the chapter doesn't recommend.

    8. A parent wants to set up a reward for their child after completing a long, boring homework session, following this chapter's reward system design principles. Which approach fits best?¶

    1. Promise a large reward at the end of the school year for consistent effort
    2. Offer a small, immediate reward right after the session, written on the list in advance, and actually deliver it every time
    3. Only mention the reward if the child specifically asks for one
    4. Give the reward randomly, sometimes before the work and sometimes long after, to keep it interesting
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter's redesign calls for rewards that are immediate and small, visible in advance, and actually collected every time — distant rewards don't motivate an ADHD reward system. Option A relies on exactly the distant-reward structure the chapter says fails. Option C removes the advance visibility the chapter treats as essential. Option D introduces unreliable timing that would teach the system the reward is fake.

    9. A person set up a complete distraction-blocking system in September, and by November it has completely fallen apart — blockers uninstalled, notifications back on, ritual abandoned. Based on this chapter's guidance, what should they do?¶

    1. Conclude the whole approach is worthless and never try any system again
    2. Rebuild the exact same elaborate system from scratch with no changes
    3. Hold a "cheerful funeral" for the collapsed system, harvest the parts that worked, and rebuild a smaller version while adding a missing weekly maintenance review
    4. Blame themselves for lacking the discipline to maintain any system
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter's exact answer to this scenario is to hold a cheerful funeral, keep what worked, rebuild shrunk, and add the missing weekly maintenance review so the next decay gets caught early. Option A overgeneralizes one collapse into total failure. Option B ignores the lesson that rebuilding smaller, not identically, is the effective move. Option D revives the character-blame framing the chapter explicitly rejects.

    10. A family notices that whenever one of their household systems (a chore chart, a calendar, a blocker) stops working, they immediately conclude "we're just not organized people" and give up on systems entirely for months. Using this chapter's analysis of system maintenance, what is the deeper flaw in their response?¶

    1. They are treating normal, expected system decay as a permanent character verdict, when the chapter's framing is that systems are perishable and maintenance — not personal failure — is what keeps a household running long-term
    2. They are correct; repeated system failure proves the family isn't capable of being organized
    3. The only solution is to hire a professional organizer instead of building any systems themselves
    4. Digital systems would solve this problem permanently, since they never decay
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter's whole argument is that systems are perishable by design, and the households that stay functional are the ones that treat maintenance as normal Tuesday work rather than reading each collapse as a character verdict. Option B accepts the flawed conclusion the chapter is directly arguing against. Option C skips past the maintenance skill the chapter says any family can learn. Option D contradicts the chapter's explicit statement that even digital tools like calendars can decay without upkeep, just less easily.
