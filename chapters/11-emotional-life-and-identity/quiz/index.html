    Quiz: Emotional Life And Identity¶

    Test your understanding of emotional dysregulation, shame, grief, identity, and the skills for steering the storm with these review questions.

    1. What is emotional dysregulation, as this chapter defines it?¶

    1. A rare complication that only affects a small minority of people with ADHD
    2. The complete absence of emotional response
    3. A formal DSM-5 diagnostic category separate from ADHD
    4. Emotions that arrive faster, hit harder, and take longer to release than the situation calls for
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Emotional dysregulation is emotions that arrive faster, hit harder, and take longer to release than the situation calls for — the chapter treats it as part of ADHD's core machinery, not a side effect. Option A understates how common and central it is. Option B describes the opposite of dysregulation. Option C misplaces it as a separate DSM category rather than a feature of ADHD itself.

    Concept Tested: Emotional Dysregulation

    See: Chapter 11

    2. What is rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD)?¶

    1. A community-coined term for disproportionate, sometimes physically painful emotional collapse in response to rejection, criticism, or perceived failure
    2. A formal DSM-5 diagnosis that always accompanies ADHD
    3. A medication side effect specific to non-stimulants
    4. The clinical term for hyperactivity in adults
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. RSD is a community-coined term, not a formal diagnosis, describing a disproportionate emotional collapse in response to rejection, criticism, or even perceived or imagined failure, arriving as fact rather than as a thought. Option B wrongly treats it as an official diagnosis. Option C misplaces it as a medication effect. Option D confuses it with an unrelated symptom category.

    Concept Tested: Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria

    See: Chapter 11

    3. How does this chapter distinguish guilt from shame?¶

    1. Guilt and shame are simply two words for the same experience
    2. Guilt only occurs in children, while shame only occurs in adults
    3. Guilt says "I did a bad thing" and tends to repair, while shame says "I am the bad thing" and tends to corrode, hide, and avoid
    4. Shame is always a healthy motivator, while guilt is always harmful
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter draws a sharp line: guilt is about an action and drives repair, while shame is about identity and drives hiding, avoidance, and corrosion — illustrated by the unopened mail and the unstarted project. Option A collapses a distinction the chapter treats as essential. Option B invents an age restriction not in the text. Option D reverses the chapter's actual evaluation of the two emotions.

    Concept Tested: Shame And ADHD

    See: Chapter 11

    4. Why does "pausing before reacting" work as a regulation skill, according to this chapter?¶

    1. Because thinking about a problem for an hour always resolves it completely
    2. Because pausing eliminates the possibility of ever feeling upset again
    3. Because a pause guarantees the triggering concern was imaginary
    4. Because an emotional surge typically peaks and begins to fall within roughly ninety seconds if it isn't re-fueled by further action, so buying that time lets it decay
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter explains the surge peaks and begins falling within about ninety seconds if not re-fueled by action like sending an angry text, so mechanical tools like the drafts-folder rule exist purely to buy that window. Option A overstates what a pause accomplishes. Option B claims an impossible permanent effect. Option C wrongly assumes the underlying concern is always false, which the chapter explicitly says isn't the point.

    Concept Tested: Pausing Before Reacting

    See: Chapter 11

    5. According to this chapter, why does self-compassion improve follow-through rather than lowering standards?¶

    1. Because shame drives hiding and avoidance, while decency and self-compassion leave enough safety for a person to try again
    2. Because self-compassion has no measurable effect on behavior at all
    3. Because self-compassion means never holding oneself accountable for anything
    4. Because self-compassion only works for people without ADHD
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter cites research showing self-compassion improves follow-through because shame drives hiding and avoidance, while decency toward oneself leaves enough psychological safety to try again. Option B contradicts the evidence the chapter cites. Option C confuses self-compassion with the absence of accountability, which the chapter never claims. Option D invents an unsupported restriction.

    Concept Tested: Self-Compassion

    See: Chapter 11

    6. What does this chapter identify as the two common failure modes in "identity after diagnosis," and what is the more stable landing between them?¶

    1. The failure modes are laziness and overachievement; the stable landing is perfect balance between the two
    2. The failure modes are over-merger (ADHD becomes the whole identity) and rejection (refusing the label entirely); the stable landing treats ADHD as load-bearing context, not the whole self
    3. The only failure mode is refusing treatment; the stable landing is always taking medication
    4. The failure modes only apply to children, never to adults
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter names over-merger and rejection as the two sandbars, with the stable landing being ADHD as load-bearing context — one important fact among many, rather than the whole identity. Option A invents unrelated failure modes. Option C narrows identity work to a treatment choice, which is a separate topic. Option D is false; the chapter discusses this process primarily in the context of late-diagnosed adults.

    7. A friend texts back a brief "ok" and the recipient immediately feels certain the friendship is over. Using this chapter's cognitive reframing skill, what is the recommended response?¶

    1. Immediately confront the friend about ending the friendship
    2. Assume the catastrophic reading is definitely correct, since RSD alarms are always accurate
    3. Generate at least one alternative reading (such as "she's driving") and hold both possibilities until real evidence arrives, rather than acting on the catastrophic interpretation alone
    4. Avoid all future contact with the friend to prevent further hurt
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Cognitive reframing means generating one alternative reading and holding both interpretations as hypotheses until evidence arrives, exactly the "ok" text example the chapter uses. Option A acts on the catastrophic reading without checking it. Option B contradicts the chapter's point that RSD alarms are false at least half the time. Option D overcorrects into avoidance, which the chapter never recommends.

    Concept Tested: Cognitive Reframing

    See: Chapter 11

    8. Someone wants to ask their manager for deadlines instead of vague timelines, using this chapter's "mechanism → pattern → ask" formula for explaining their ADHD. Which statement best follows that formula?¶

    1. "I have ADHD, which is a neurodevelopmental disorder defined by inattention and hyperactivity-impulsivity that begins in childhood."
    2. "My brain doesn't cue me at the right moment, which is why I miss things I care about — deadlines land better than 'whenever' for me. Could you give me a real date?"
    3. "Sorry to bother you, but I sometimes struggle with things, so please be patient with me in general."
    4. "I can't help forgetting things, so it's not really my responsibility if deadlines get missed."
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. This is the chapter's own worked formula — one sentence of mechanism, one of pattern, one specific ask — delivered without apology or medical lecture. Option A is a clinical definition, not a mechanism-pattern-ask statement suited to a workplace conversation. Option C is vague and apologetic, offering no specific ask. Option D deflects responsibility rather than requesting a concrete accommodation.

    9. During an argument, a person feels a surge of anger, says "I'm taking ten," and leaves the room, having agreed on this protocol with their partner in advance. The partner follows them, saying "we're not done — you always run away." Using this chapter's framing, how should this exchange be evaluated?¶

    1. The person leaving is doing the skill correctly by buying time for the surge to decay; the partner's pursuit undermines the mechanism, though their need for the conversation to resume afterward is legitimate and should be honored when the pause ends
    2. Both people handled the moment equally well
    3. The person leaving is avoiding responsibility and should be required to stay and argue it out
    4. The partner is right to follow, since walking away from any argument is always harmful
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter's own worked example grades this exact exchange this way: the pre-agreed pause is the skill working and should be praised, while pursuit refuels the surge and punishes the skill — though the conversation must genuinely resume afterward for the protocol to hold trust. Option B ignores that one move helps and the other harms. Option C mislabels a regulation skill as avoidance. Option D overgeneralizes a specific pre-agreed protocol into a universal rule about leaving arguments.

    Concept Tested: Pausing Before Reacting

    10. A person who was recently diagnosed with ADHD finds that nearly every conversation, hobby, and social media feed they engage with now revolves entirely around ADHD content, several months after diagnosis. Using this chapter's analysis of identity after diagnosis and online ADHD culture, what is the best way to understand this pattern?¶

    1. This proves the diagnosis must be wrong, since a real diagnosis wouldn't take over someone's whole identity
    2. This is a permanent and unavoidable state that everyone with ADHD experiences forever
    3. This likely reflects the common "over-merger" phase, often amplified by algorithmic feeds that reward engagement with ADHD content, and it typically settles over time as the person integrates ADHD as one part of a larger identity
    4. Online ADHD content is entirely unreliable and should be avoided completely
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter names over-merger as a common, usually temporary phase, and notes that algorithmic feeds amplify it by making ADHD content someone's whole scroll once engagement is detected — it typically settles into ADHD as one part of a larger identity. Option A misreads a common phase as proof against the diagnosis. Option B contradicts the chapter's description of over-merger as usually temporary. Option D overcorrects; the chapter values online community for validation and tactics while cautioning only about medical claims.
