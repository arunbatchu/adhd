    Applied Quiz: Emotional Life And Identity¶

    A second set of questions on this chapter, weighted toward applying the ideas rather than recalling them. Several put you inside a storm and ask what helps.

    1. A man detonates over a dropped fork at 6 pm after handling a genuine crisis calmly that morning. What explains the contrast?¶

    1. The morning crisis was less upsetting than it appeared
    2. The evening event carried hidden significance in the relationship
    3. He is more emotionally invested in home life than in work
    4. A weak brake on feelings plus a drained regulation battery — the ADHD day spends load faster, so evening surges get through
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Emotional dysregulation is core machinery, not a side effect: Chapter 2's response inhibition brakes feelings on their way to expression, so the same weak brake that lets the blurted comment through lets the surge through — and the fried-battery effect explains the timing. The chapter adds two facts that change how households read these storms: the reaction is real rather than theatrical, so "you're overreacting" is technically true and completely useless, and nobody chooses the surge, only what happens next.

    See: Chapter 11

    2. After a blowup, he is genuinely fine in ten minutes and puzzled that his wife is still shaken an hour later. What does the chapter say causes the most family damage here?¶

    1. The asymmetry itself — he's moved on and she hasn't — which is why naming it out loud beats any apology protocol
    2. The intensity of the original outburst
    3. His failure to apologize thoroughly enough
    4. Her unwillingness to move past it at the same pace
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. ADHD anger is typically a squall, not a front: sudden, intense, and often over in minutes. The chapter says that asymmetry causes more family damage than the squall itself, and offers the specific move — "I know you're past it; I need ten more minutes." Options C and D assign fault to one side, while the chapter treats the mismatch as a mechanical fact that both people can name rather than litigate.

    3. A woman reads a one-word "ok" text and is instantly certain the friendship is over. Her partner responds "all I said was—" and things get worse. What should he do instead?¶

    1. Present evidence that her interpretation is factually wrong
    2. Offer reassurance in the moment — "you're not in trouble; I'm not going anywhere" — and argue with the feeling's size later
    3. Leave the room until the spike passes
    4. Ask her to explain why the text upset her so much
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. RSD's practical signature is speed and totality — it doesn't feel like a thought, it arrives as fact, in the body, at full volume. The chapter's family instruction is exact: during a spike, reassurance lands and litigation escalates. Options A and D are both forms of litigation, asking her to defend or justify a surge she didn't choose. The chapter also notes why the alarm runs hot: it was trained on decades of real criticism.

    Concept Tested: Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria

    See: Chapter 11

    4. A two-minute task — opening a piece of mail — has been avoided for two days. What does the chapter say this signals?¶

    1. A working-memory failure, since the task was likely forgotten
    2. Ordinary procrastination requiring a smaller first step
    3. Shame mechanics, not logistics — each envelope is a possible indictment, and starting risks confirming the verdict
    4. Poor prioritization, since the task is objectively trivial
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter gives this as the tell for both readers: when a two-minute task produces a two-day avoidance, you're not looking at logistics anymore. Shame says I am the bad thing where guilt says I did a bad thing — guilt repairs, shame hides. It is also the engine behind behaviors families misread as not-caring, including deflecting with jokes or anger when a slip is mentioned, because the correction touched the wound rather than the event.

    5. Eight months post-diagnosis, with medication settled and systems running, a woman still dreads opening email. Why hasn't the toolkit fixed it?¶

    1. Systems fix Tuesdays; the twenty-year file needs its own work
    2. Her medication dose requires further adjustment
    3. Her systems are not yet mature enough to have taken effect
    4. Email avoidance indicates a co-occurring anxiety disorder
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Sana's example threads the whole section: every "quick question" from her boss detonates the RSD alarm, every small slip gets processed as identity, every success gets credited to the mask. Her ADHD is treated; the sediment isn't — yet. The chapter says this distinction is exactly why it sits between the toolkit chapters and the relationship chapters. Option D is possible in general but isn't what the example demonstrates.

    6. Someone's inner narrator says "you idiot, you forgot again." What test does the chapter offer, and what does the research say about the alternative?¶

    1. Ignore the thought entirely, since inner narration cannot be changed
    2. Accept it as accurate accountability, since standards must be maintained
    3. Replace it with positive affirmations regardless of accuracy
    4. Ask "would you say it to a friend?" — and note that self-compassion improves follow-through while shame degrades it
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The substitution test cuts through the fluff: your friend forgot because her prospective memory misfires, so you'd say "your alarm system failed you; want help building a better one." The chapter anticipates the skeptic's objection with the research finding — self-compassion is not lowered standards, because shame drives hiding and avoidance while decency leaves enough safety to try again. Mechanically, it's Chapter 10's maintenance stance applied to a person: audit the system, not the soul.

    7. A month after her late diagnosis, a woman is grieving. Her family says "at least you know now." What is wrong with that?¶

    1. Nothing — reframing toward the positive is what grief needs
    2. It converts a mourner into someone who now also feels wrong about mourning
    3. It is accurate but should be delivered by a clinician
    4. It understates how much the diagnosis will change
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter calls this the one wrong move and notes families keep making it with love. The grief is real grief — for the decades misfiled, the report cards, the relationships and jobs that broke on unnamed mechanisms — and it moves like grief, in waves, coexisting with genuine relief, with both correct and neither cancelling the other. What it needs is acknowledgment, time, sometimes a therapist, and a family that doesn't rush it.

    8. A year after diagnosis, a man routes every trait, choice, and conflict through ADHD, and talks about little else. How does the chapter characterize this?¶

    1. Over-merger — a known and usually temporary sandbar, with the stable landing being ADHD as load-bearing context rather than core
    2. A healthy and permanent integration of the diagnosis
    3. Evidence that the diagnosis was incorrect
    4. Rejection of the label in disguised form
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter charts two failure modes like sandbars: over-merger, usually a year-one phase amplified by online spaces, and rejection, where refusing the label means the shame file stays open under old management. Most people land somewhere specific — "I have a brain with a distinctive steering system; it explains a lot of my history; it's one fact about me among many" — and that reframing is literally the first section of Chapter 15's Owner's Manual.

    9. Someone wants to stop sending messages they regret. What does the chapter say the whole game is, and which move fits?¶

    1. Build the habit of rereading messages once before sending
    2. Buy ninety seconds — the surge peaks and falls within roughly that window if it isn't re-fueled — using a pre-decided rule like writing the message and saving it for an hour
    3. Avoid written communication during emotional periods entirely
    4. Rely on improved self-control as regulation skills develop
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Pausing before reacting is the master skill, and the chapter frames it as engineering rather than virtue. The pre-decided mechanical buys include the drafts folder rule (the message is always calmer or unnecessary in an hour), the leave-the-room protocol agreed in advance, the physical dump since the surge is somatic, and mindfulness reps that create the gap. None of these stop the surge arriving; they widen what happens between the surge and the world.

    Concept Tested: Pausing Before Reacting

    10. Someone wants to explain their ADHD to a manager without lecturing or apologizing. What formula does the chapter give?¶

    1. Lead with the diagnosis, then describe the science in enough depth to be credible
    2. Avoid mechanism entirely and simply request the accommodation
    3. Mechanism → pattern → ask: one sentence on how the brain works, one on what it looks like in practice, one specific request
    4. Describe the history of struggle first, so the request is understood in context
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter's worked version runs: "My brain doesn't cue me at the right moment — that's why I miss things I care about. Deadlines land better than 'whenever.' Could you give me a real date?" No apology, no lecture, no medical seminar — a user's guide excerpt delivered in twenty seconds. That formula, expanded and written down, is the Personal Owner's Manual, and the chapter recommends drafting three versions — for a friend, a boss, and a partner — as its single best exercise.
