    Applied Quiz: Work And Career¶

    A second set of questions on this chapter, weighted toward applying the ideas rather than recalling them. Each one puts you in a working situation and asks what the chapter would do.

    1. A nurse fails in a records-and-compliance role, concludes she is "bad at working," and nearly leaves the profession — then thrives in an ER. What changed?¶

    1. Nothing about her ADHD changed; the water did — the ER pays for exactly what her brain volunteers
    2. Emergency medicine is objectively easier than records work
    3. She developed better coping skills in the interval
    4. The second role had more supportive management
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Same credential, same person, opposite performance reviews. The chapter's inversion is the key move: don't ask "what's wrong with me at this job?" but "what does this job pay for, and is it what my brain produces?" The ER asks for triage urgency, novelty by the hour, visible immediate results, movement, and a team — while the compliance role was a daily stack of Chapter 3's worst task shapes.

    2. Someone finds an article titled "Top 10 Careers for People with ADHD." How should they treat it?¶

    1. Follow it, since the research on ADHD career matching is well established
    2. Skeptically — fit is a shape, not a list, and the genre is astrology with a salary column
    3. Follow it only for the roles that also pay well
    4. Ignore career fit entirely and optimize for salary
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter names the shapes that fit — built-in urgency or deadline rhythm, novelty and variation, visible immediate results, movement, and interest-alignment strong enough to survive boring stretches — and the shapes that fight, like long-horizon self-scheduled work and high-volume administrative precision. It also adds two honest caveats: every job carries an admin tax, so the question is the rate rather than its existence, and fit can often be retrofitted.

    3. Someone hates 30% of their job and is preparing to quit. What does the chapter suggest first?¶

    1. Quit promptly, since partial fit predicts eventual failure
    2. Endure it, since every job contains unpleasant work
    3. Try renegotiating the role's shape — trading tasks with a detail-loving colleague, moving to the customer-facing side, volunteering for the urgent-response rotation
    4. Request a formal ADA accommodation to remove those duties entirely
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter's line is that the job is often wrong by 30%, not 100%, and 30% is negotiable. Option D overshoots: the ADA requires that you perform the job's essential functions with or without accommodation — it levels the field, it doesn't waive the job — so requesting the removal of core duties misunderstands the statute.

    4. A job seeker with ADHD is applying sporadically, spending hours perfecting each application, and taking silence hard. What does the chapter prescribe?¶

    1. Apply only to roles where an inside contact can advocate for them
    2. Perfect fewer applications, since quality determines outcomes
    3. Wait until motivation returns before resuming
    4. Run it as a project — daily next-actions like "send two applications," body doubling, timeboxing, and a pre-decided reframe that a non-reply is a statistic, not a verdict
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The search itself is a hostile task shape: a long, unstructured, rejection-rich project with distant payoffs, run entirely on self-scheduling — essentially a stress test of everything Chapter 2 called hardest. The chapter names the RSD hazard explicitly and says volume is the strategy. It also offers encouragement: interview prep plays to strengths, being novel, social, high-urgency, and improvisational, so many people with ADHD interview far better than they apply.

    5. An employee wants written follow-ups after verbal instructions and a protected morning focus block. Must they disclose a diagnosis?¶

    1. Not necessarily — most accommodations are things a good manager would grant any employee who asked well, so ask for the specific condition rather than presenting the diagnosis
    2. Yes, since ADA protection is a precondition for any accommodation
    3. Yes, and it must go through HR in writing
    4. No, because employers are required to provide these to everyone
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter calls this the practical secret of workplace accommodations: the common menu is Chapters 9-10 translated into HR language, and many items can be obtained informally. The formal ADA process is the tool you escalate to when informal asking fails or when stakes demand paper. Option D overstates — these aren't universal entitlements, they're reasonable requests a good manager often grants.

    6. Someone is weighing whether to disclose their ADHD at work. What does the chapter say about timing, and what path do most people take?¶

    1. Disclose immediately upon diagnosis, since honesty is the foundation of the employment relationship
    2. Never disclose, since stigma outweighs any protection gained
    3. Graduated disclosure — informal specific asks first, partial disclosure if needed, full formal disclosure reserved for when the ADA's machinery is required — decided before a crisis rather than after a bad review
    4. Disclose only after a performance problem, when the protection is actually needed
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter frames disclosure as a strategic decision with real trade-offs, not a moral test of self-acceptance — Chapter 11's identity work does not obligate anyone to tell their boss anything. Timing matters because protection works best before a performance crisis, since disclosure after a bad review can read as excuse rather than explanation, which is why option D inverts the advice. This is also one of two places the chapter says the family reader genuinely helps: as rehearsal audience and devil's advocate, then respecting the decision.

    7. Someone constantly interrupts in meetings and feels rude about it. What does the chapter recommend?¶

    1. Park the blurts on paper — writing the interjection holds the thought so working memory can release it, which is most of why interruption happens
    2. Speak only when directly addressed
    3. Sit further from the discussion to reduce the impulse
    4. Rehearse contributions in advance of each meeting
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter identifies the mechanism rather than moralizing: interruption happens partly because an unspoken thought is a thought likely lost, so writing it down solves the underlying problem. The rest of the meeting craft: bring a legal fidget, since doodling and note-taking are socially invisible movement that genuinely aids listening; capture actions in real time as they're assigned; and where you have influence, shape the meeting itself with agendas in advance and a hard stop.

    8. An employee is given a project due in six weeks. What converts it into something their motivation system can act on?¶

    1. Setting private milestones and tracking them personally
    2. Sending the manager a stage plan with dates, which manufactures institutional witnesses and turns one distant deadline into three near ones
    3. Starting immediately and working steadily throughout
    4. Asking for the deadline to be shortened
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Self-imposed checkpoints don't bind because there is no witness — that's Chapter 10's rule applied at work. The chapter also notes the career side effect and calls it the recurring irony of the section: the person who ships stage plans reads as exceptionally organized, so ADHD-driven workarounds, externally visible, look like elite professionalism. Option A is precisely the version without a witness.

    9. A performance review contains one criticism — "be more organized" — and the employee spirals. What are the two moves the chapter gives?¶

    1. Accept the feedback without response and address it privately later
    2. Defend the work in the meeting so the record is corrected
    3. Buy the ninety seconds by taking notes and asking clarifying questions, then request specifics — "can you give me an example?" turns a global criticism into an engineerable one
    4. Request that future feedback be delivered in writing only
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. This is Chapter 11's RSD kit wearing a lanyard: writing is a legal pause, holding two readings means sorting each item into "fix" versus "feeling" an hour later, and the drafts-folder rule governs any written response. The workplace-specific addition is requesting specifics, which converts "be more organized" into "the Henderson file was late twice" — and engineerable is home turf. The chapter also recommends asking for frequent small feedback rather than annual and huge, since the RSD load per correction is smaller.

    10. A family member wants an early warning sign that a working adult with ADHD is heading for burnout. What should they watch?¶

    1. Declining output quality on major projects
    2. Increased irritability at home in the evenings
    3. Rising sleep complaints
    4. That the enthusiasm has stopped — not the energy — because an ADHD adult who has stopped being excited by novel things is redlining
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter calls this the family reader's second real job in the chapter, and specifies the distinction carefully: not the energy, the enthusiasm. The person who notices has standing to say so, gently, before the collapse does. This matters because burnout announces itself late and Chapter 3's interoception is unreliable — which is also why the prevention kit calendars recovery rather than waiting to feel tired, and enforces a yes-budget by calendar rather than in-the-moment judgment.
