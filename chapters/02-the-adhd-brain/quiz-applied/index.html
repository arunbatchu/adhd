    Applied Quiz: The ADHD Brain¶

    A second set of questions on this chapter, weighted toward applying the ideas rather than recalling them. Each one gives you a situation and asks which piece of the machinery explains it.

    1. A manager says: "Before the meeting, print the deck, use Dana's updated numbers, and book the small room." The employee arrives with the deck, old numbers, no room. Which executive function was overloaded?¶

    1. Working memory — three sequential verbal instructions exceeded the mental desk, so the later items shoved out the earlier ones
    2. Response inhibition — she failed to suppress a competing action
    3. Cognitive flexibility — she could not shift between the three sub-tasks
    4. Self-monitoring — she did not notice she was drifting off task
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. This is the chapter's Priya example. Working memory is the small workspace holding information while you use it, and three sequential spoken instructions exceeded it — the second shoved out the first, a hallway greeting shoved out the third. The information was never filed, so this isn't forgetting in the filing-cabinet sense. Options B, C, and D name real executive functions, but none describes an overloaded capacity limit. Note the practical consequence: the fix is never "listen harder," it's writing things down as they arrive, because paper has no capacity limit.

    Concept Tested: Working Memory

    See: Chapter 2

    2. A student does a hard assignment due tomorrow in ninety focused minutes, but leaves a bigger assignment due in three weeks untouched for twenty days — then does it in ninety focused minutes the night before. What does reward-math conclude?¶

    1. The student is unmotivated, and the two sprints were flukes
    2. The student has a skills gap that panic temporarily closes
    3. The two assignments differed in difficulty despite appearances
    4. The same brain behaved consistently — it moved when the payoff came close enough to enter motivational range
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter uses exactly this case to contrast effort-theory with reward-math. Under effort-theory the student looks unmotivated, except the two identical ninety-minute sprints prove otherwise. Under reward-math both performances are the same brain behaving consistently: the only variable was when the consequence arrived. Deadlines don't create the ability — they move the reward close enough to see. Options A and B misread the sprints as exceptions rather than as the evidence. Option C contradicts the setup, which holds difficulty constant.

    Concept Tested: Delay Aversion

    See: Chapter 2

    3. An adult writes well and handles hard emails in the morning, then by late afternoon rereads paragraphs, snaps at a coworker, and impulse-buys a gadget. What is the best explanation?¶

    1. Two different conditions are at work, one affecting mornings and one affecting afternoons
    2. Prefrontal work is expensive and depletes with fatigue, so the same person is running on a drained executive battery
    3. Her ADHD is intermittent, which rules out a neurodevelopmental explanation
    4. The afternoon behavior reflects a character problem the morning conceals
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Every item on that list — sustained attention, inhibiting the snap, resisting the impulse buy — is prefrontal, and the prefrontal cortex is the brain's heaviest lifting, easily depleted by fatigue, stress, hunger, and poor sleep. As the chapter puts it about Aisha: the machine didn't change, the fuel gauge did. Option D is the character reading the chapter is written to replace. Option A invents a second condition. Option C mistakes a predictable daily pattern for evidence against a lifelong condition.

    Concept Tested: Prefrontal Cortex

    See: Chapter 2

    4. Parents of a 13-year-old point out that his classmates manage their own backpacks. Using the maturation-lag finding, what is the flaw in that comparison?¶

    1. There is no flaw — same-age peers are the correct benchmark
    2. The comparison is unfair because backpack management is not an executive function
    3. They are right that peers manage it, but wrong about what hardware he is running — the organizing circuitry may be on a schedule closer to a 10-year-old's
    4. Brain scans could settle the question for this specific child
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter's Leo example makes exactly this move: his body, vocabulary, and height are 13, so everyone bills him for 13-year-old self-management, but the circuitry that inhibits impulses and organizes homework may run on a younger schedule. The useful question becomes what scaffolding bridges the gap while it narrows. Option D is ruled out explicitly — the lag is a group average, and no brain scan can diagnose ADHD in an individual. Option B is wrong: organizing a backpack is planning and working memory.

    Concept Tested: ADHD Brain Maturation Lag

    See: Chapter 2

    5. A clinic advertises brain scans that "diagnose ADHD definitively." How should a family evaluate this offer?¶

    1. Decline — neuroimaging findings in ADHD are consistent at the group level, but no scan can diagnose an individual, so the clinic is selling past the evidence
    2. Accept, since imaging is more objective than rating scales and interviews
    3. Accept only if the scan is read alongside a genetic test
    4. Decline, because no reliable group-level brain differences have been found in ADHD
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter says this plainly and points readers back to Chapter 1's five-question filter. Group-level findings are real — the maturation delay, small average volume differences in childhood, differences in network coordination — but they do not transfer to individual diagnosis. Option D overcorrects and denies findings that genuinely exist. Options B and C treat imaging as more authoritative than the clinical assessment described in Chapter 5.

    Concept Tested: Neuroimaging Findings

    See: Chapter 2

    6. A child rocks his chair during silent reading, an adult works best in a noisy café, and a teen saves homework for midnight adrenaline. What single mechanism links all three?¶

    1. Avoidance — each is a way of escaping a disliked task
    2. Response inhibition failing in three different settings
    3. Poor sleep hygiene expressing itself at different ages
    4. Arousal regulation — each is adding stimulation to climb out of under-arousal, self-medicating with the tools at hand
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter reframes all three as self-regulation rather than misbehavior: ADHD brains hold the alert-but-organized zone poorly and slide toward under-arousal, where boredom borders on physical discomfort. Chapter 9 turns this from accident into design — chosen background noise, movement breaks, stimulation on purpose instead of by emergency. Option A misreads deliberate stimulation-seeking as avoidance. Option B names the brake, which isn't what's operating. Option C explains only the midnight case, and not why café noise helps.

    Concept Tested: Arousal Regulation

    See: Chapter 2

    7. A reader is midway through a page and suddenly realizes they have spent three minutes mentally rewriting an argument from 2019, with no memory of leaving the page. Which finding names this?¶

    1. Working memory overflow during reading
    2. Unreliable suppression of the default mode network, so the brain's screensaver flickers back on mid-task
    3. Delay aversion applied to long-form text
    4. A failure of the brake, since the thought should have been inhibited
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. On-task brains normally suppress the default mode network — the network handling daydreaming, remembering, and replaying conversations — and in ADHD that suppression is less reliable. The chapter offers this exact experience as the inside view. It also notes the flip side: that idling network is where unexpected connections and creative leaps come from, a thread Chapter 11 picks up. Options A, C, and D name mechanisms that don't describe an intruding internal-thought network.

    Concept Tested: Default Mode Network

    See: Chapter 2

    8. A family wants to motivate a teenager toward a chore that is important but dull. Which approach fits the interest-based nervous system?¶

    1. Explain more clearly how important the chore is and what depends on it
    2. Increase the consequence for not doing it, so the obligation registers
    3. Convert the chore into a currency the system pays out for — make it novel, urgent, shared, or competitive
    4. Wait until the teenager matures enough for importance to work
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter's practical translation is to stop asking "how important is this?" — a currency the system barely accepts — and start asking how it can become interesting, novel, urgent, or shared. Body doubling, gamified chores, and artificial deadlines are all currency exchanges, and Chapter 9 is built from them. Options A and B keep paying in the importance currency that doesn't convert. Option D misreads a lifespan pattern as something waiting to resolve on its own.

    Concept Tested: Interest-Based Nervous System

    See: Chapter 2

    9. A teenager sleeps five hours nightly during exam season and her ADHD symptoms visibly worsen. What does the chapter's two-directional account predict?¶

    1. Sleep loss adds tiredness but leaves ADHD symptoms themselves unchanged
    2. The worsening indicates the ADHD diagnosis was probably wrong
    3. Sleep and ADHD are largely independent, so the timing is coincidental
    4. Sleep loss degrades the same prefrontal functions ADHD already strains, so it doesn't just add tiredness — it amplifies ADHD itself
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The relationship runs both ways: ADHD disrupts sleep (delayed body clock, racing default mode network at lights-out, revenge bedtime procrastination), and poor sleep degrades working memory, inhibition, and emotional control — exactly the functions already under strain. The chapter adds a detail worth carrying: severe sleep deprivation can even mimic ADHD in people who don't have it, which is why competent evaluators always ask about sleep. Options A and C deny the amplification. Option B inverts the logic.

    Concept Tested: Sleep And The ADHD Brain

    See: Chapter 2

    10. Two adults both have ADHD. One cannot begin tasks but manages instructions easily; the other starts instantly but cannot let go of a failed approach. What does this tell you about strategy?¶

    1. One of them has been misdiagnosed, since ADHD presents consistently
    2. Executive functions are distinct, so profiles differ — and strategies attach to specific components rather than to "ADHD" in general
    3. The difference reflects effort, not machinery
    4. They need identical strategies, since both have the same underlying condition
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter states this directly before the explorer: executive functions are distinct, so one person's working memory can be fine while initiation is wrecked, and another launches instantly but can't shift gears. This is why Chapter 15's Personal Owner's Manual asks readers to map their own profile, and why the strategies in Chapters 9 and 10 attach to named components. A backpack problem that is working memory needs a launchpad by the door; one that is task initiation needs a smaller first step. Options A and D assume a uniformity the chapter denies; option C returns to the effort explanation.
