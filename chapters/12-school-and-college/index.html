    School And College¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter covers learning with ADHD from study strategies through formal accommodations: IEPs and 504 plans in school, disability services in college, and how to actually request help. It is written for the student and for the parent watching from the sidelines. After this chapter, readers know what support exists and how to get it.

    School is where ADHD gets graded — literally, daily, for sixteen-plus years. It's also where the most formal support infrastructure exists, most of it unused by the families who need it, usually because nobody explained what exists or how to ask. This chapter does both jobs: the private toolkit (study techniques re-engineered for the brain from Chapter 2) and the public one (the legal machinery of accommodations, from second grade through graduate school). A note on scope: the legal specifics here are US-based (IDEA, Section 504, the ADA); other countries have parallel structures with different names, and the strategy layer travels everywhere.

    For the parent reading this: your role changes across this chapter — heavy lifting in elementary school, coaching from nearby in high school (Chapter 4's transfer-before-the-cliff), and, by college, nothing without invitation. Every section marks where the handoff stands.

    Studying: Techniques That Fit the Machine¶

    Study strategies for ADHD start from an honest diagnosis of the default method: rereading and highlighting — the strategies most students use — are passive, and passive input is exactly what an interest-based attention system slides off (Chapter 3's mind wandering: eyes finish the page, nothing lands). The replacement principle across every technique below: make studying an activity with feedback rather than an exposure. Active methods aren't just better pedagogy — they're better ADHD pedagogy, because activity supplies the engagement, urgency, and immediate feedback the reward system runs on (Chapter 2). What follows is each core technique, its mechanism, and its ADHD adaptation.

    Active recall is the highest-value technique in the research and the best-fitting for this brain: instead of re-reading material, close the book and retrieve it — flashcards, practice problems, blank-page brain dumps, explaining the concept aloud to the dog. Retrieval is a test with instant feedback (got it / didn't), which makes it self-gamifying — and it exposes the Chapter 3 illusion rereading creates, where familiarity ("I've seen this") impersonates knowledge ("I can produce this"). Spaced repetition schedules those retrievals at growing intervals — reviewing just as forgetting begins — and its ADHD adaptation is blunt: don't run the schedule on prospective memory (Chapter 2 told you how that ends); use an app that fires (the flashcard tools that schedule reviews automatically are Chapter 10's fire-not-wait rule applied to studying), and accept that cramming — the deadline-fueled all-nighter — is the discount curve doing what it does; spaced tools with alarms are how the material gets met before the panic window.

    Note-taking systems have one ADHD-specific truth the productivity industry resists: the perfect notes are the ones that keep you processing, not transcribing. Verbatim transcription is passive input with a pen. The adaptations that work: structured skeletons (headings pre-written from the syllabus, so notes have slots to land in), the split-page method (notes on one side, questions-to-retrieve-later on the other — feeding active recall directly), permission to be ugly (notes are working memory's external drive, not a deliverable), and — where allowed — photographing the board while listening, because divided attention costs this brain double (Chapter 1's dividing). Reading with ADHD applies the activity principle to text: convert reading into a search task by writing three questions the section should answer before starting (giving the spotlight a target), read with something in hand (pen margin-notes, or a finger-drag pace-setter for the sliding-eyes problem), timebox with sprints (a chapter is a project; 20 minutes is a task — Chapter 9's Pomodoro), and use audio legitimately (text-to-speech or audiobooks-while-following-along keep the channel busy enough to hold; many people with ADHD absorb spoken text far better, and that's a tool, not a cheat). Writing with ADHD is mostly a task-initiation and perfectionism problem wearing an academic costume, and the fixes come from Chapters 9 and 11: separate the ugly draft from the edit (the essay that can't be started is usually the perfect essay that can't be started; the terrible draft has no such enemies), talk it before typing it (record a two-minute explanation to a friend, transcribe, edit — speech routes around the blank-page freeze), outline as next-actions ("write intro" is a project; "write three sentences on why the experiment mattered" is a task), and scaffold the deadline (Chapter 10: staged mini-deadlines with witnesses, because the paper due in five weeks is motivationally invisible until it's due in five hours).

    Test-taking strategies for ADHD target the two test-day failure modes that aren't knowledge problems: time blindness (the exam is a fixed-time task run without external cues — so bring the wristwatch where allowed, write a per-section time budget on the exam's margin at minute zero, and set checkpoint questions: "at halfway, I should be at question 20") and impulsive answering (the blurt, in multiple-choice form — misread stems, the first plausible option grabbed; the mechanical counter is annotating the stem: circle not, underline always, and physically touch every option before committing). Add the triage pass — answer everything easy first, mark and return to the hard ones — because sunk-cost hyperfocus on question 7 is how strong students with ADHD hand back exams that are 40% blank. Homework systems close the private toolkit by pointing Chapter 9 at the backpack: the assignment must be captured at the moment of assignment (planner, phone, photo of the board — the monopoly capture point, because "I'll remember it" is the leak this system exists to plug), the work needs a launchpad-and-time anchor (same place, same after-school slot, stacked on a snack — decision-free by design), and the finished work needs the step families forget to engineer: turned in. Chapter 4's Marcus did the assignments; they died in the backpack — so the system's last link is a submission ritual (homework goes in the folder, folder goes in the bag, bag goes by the door, at the moment of completion, every time). For parents: build these systems with the student per Chapter 9's rule, and let the weekly review (Chapter 10) be the oversight mechanism rather than nightly inspection — the goal is a system the student runs by eleventh grade, because the college sections below have no parent slot in them.

    The Legal Toolkit: Accommodations in K-12¶

    School accommodations are changes to how a student learns and is assessed — not what they're expected to learn — that offset a disability's impact: extended time on tests, preferential seating, chunked instructions, reduced-repetition homework, movement breaks, a quiet testing room. The premise, which some families need permission to accept: accommodations aren't advantages — they're the ramp, not a head start. The student with ADHD taking an exam without extended time is being tested on time blindness and working memory alongside the chemistry; the accommodation removes the disability from the measurement so the chemistry can be measured. In the US, two legal vehicles deliver them, and the difference confuses everyone, so here it is clean.

    An IEP (Individualized Education Program) comes from IDEA, the special-education law: it's for students whose disability requires specialized instruction — actual changes in teaching, services (like executive-function coaching or counseling time), and measurable annual goals, with a legally mandated team, annual reviews, and parental sign-off. A 504 plan comes from Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, a civil-rights law: it's for students who don't need specialized instruction but do need access — the accommodations list above, formalized and enforceable, with a lighter process. The practical translation for ADHD families: many students with ADHD are well served by a 504 plan (access changes only); students whose ADHD comes with heavier impact or the co-occurring learning disabilities of Chapter 6 (Dev's reading disorder) may need the IEP's instructional services. Rule of thumb, not law: 504 changes the conditions, an IEP can change the teaching.

    504 Plan IEP
    Legal basis Section 504 (civil rights / access) IDEA (special education)
    Who qualifies Disability substantially limiting a major life activity (learning counts; ADHD counts) Disability requiring specialized instruction, within defined categories (ADHD usually under "Other Health Impairment")
    What it provides Accommodations: conditions, not curriculum Everything a 504 can, plus services, instruction changes, measurable goals
    Process weight Lighter: evaluation, plan, periodic review Heavier: full evaluation, team meetings, annual review, written consent
    Typical ADHD fit ADHD with primarily access needs ADHD plus learning disabilities, or heavier impairment

    Requesting accommodations is where informed families outperform, because the process rewards specificity and paper. The playbook: put the request in writing to the school (an email to the principal or counselor requesting evaluation for a 504/IEP starts legal clocks that a hallway conversation doesn't); attach the documentation you already own (Chapter 5's evaluation report — its recommendations section was written for this moment); come to the meeting with a specific, mechanism-linked list rather than "he needs help" (this is Chapter 11's mechanism → pattern → ask, formalized: "working memory limits multi-step verbal instructions → he executes step one and loses two through four → we're requesting written instructions for multi-step tasks"); expect negotiation and get the final plan in writing; and calendar the review date, because plans decay like every system in Chapter 10 — the accommodations that fit fourth grade won't fit seventh, and the plan follows the student only if someone maintains it. Two failure modes to pre-empt: the school that says "she's passing, so no plan" (passing is not the standard — the disability's impact on access is, and Chapter 4's masking section is your counter-argument: ask what the grades cost), and the plan that exists but isn't followed (the polite escalation ladder: teacher → counselor/504 coordinator → principal → district, each step in writing, each referencing the plan by name).

    A worked example of the difference process makes: Amara — Chapter 4's window-gazer — finally diagnosed in seventh grade. Version one: her mother mentions the diagnosis at conferences; the teacher promises to "keep an eye on her"; nothing changes structurally; eighth grade repeats seventh. Version two: her mother emails a written 504 evaluation request with the evaluation report attached; the meeting produces five specific accommodations (extended test time, seat away from the window, written instructions, a weekly organizational check-in with the counselor, permission to photograph the homework board); the plan is signed, distributed to all teachers, and reviewed each spring. Same child, same school, same teachers — the second version just used the machinery that existed all along. That's this chapter's entire thesis in one paragraph.

    College: Same Rights, Different Rules¶

    College disability services run on a different legal chassis (the ADA and Section 504's post-secondary provisions — IDEA and IEPs end at high school graduation), and one structural change matters more than every other detail: the student drives everything. The college will not find you, call your parents, or notice you're drowning; disability services activate only when the student self-identifies, provides documentation (that Chapter 5 evaluation report again — check the college's recency requirements before freshman year), and completes an intake conversation. Parents are legally outside this process — FERPA even keeps grades private — which is exactly why Chapter 4 insisted the systems transfer before the cliff, and why the eleventh-grade goal was a student who runs their own plan. The practical checklist for the transition summer: register with disability services before the first semester (not after the first disaster — accommodations don't apply retroactively to failed midterms), know what documentation the office requires, and rehearse the twenty-second mechanism → pattern → ask (Chapter 11), because the student will deliver it, in person, to real adults.

    College accommodations themselves will look familiar — extended test time, distraction-reduced testing rooms, note-taking support or lecture recording, priority registration (registration morning is an executive-function gauntlet; this one is underused and gold for building a schedule around Chapter 8's sleep repair) — with one operational quirk that catches students every term: accommodation letters typically must be activated — requested each term and delivered to each professor, often with a brief conversation. That step is a prospective-memory task (Chapter 2's least reliable function) gating all the others, so it goes on the calendar with an alarm, first week, every term. And the honest scope note: accommodations level the testing room; they don't supply the structure college removed (Chapter 4's cliff). The daily survival layer is Chapters 9-10 — the capture point, the calendar that fires, the body-doubled study sessions, the weekly review — now run without a household's scaffolding. Disability services plus systems is the full formula; either alone under-performs.

    Choosing a major deserves its ADHD-specific advice, because the standard advice ("follow the market") ignores the machinery: Chapter 2's interest-based nervous system makes genuine interest a structural requirement, not a luxury — the major that bores you will extract Chapter 3's procrastination tax every single week for four years, while the one that grabs you gets hyperfocus for free. The honest heuristics: weight interest heavily but audit it against Chapter 3's novelty curve (is this fascination six weeks old, or has it survived a boring stretch? — every field has boring stretches, and the question is whether this field's boring stretches are survivable); look at the assessment shape of the upper years (a major graded by constant small projects fits deadline-driven brains better than one graded by two distant monster exams — same intelligence, different scaffolding); and treat a changed major as data, not failure (it's among the most common college events there is, ADHD or not). Grad school with ADHD gets the same analysis at higher stakes: it removes even college's structure (a dissertation is one enormous, years-long, self-scheduled project — approximately the most hostile task shape this book has described), which is why the people who thrive tend to be the ones who manufacture structure deliberately — advisors who meet weekly (choose the advisor over the topic; an engaged advisor is deadline scaffolding with tenure), writing groups, lab environments with daily rhythm, and accommodations that exist at every level including doctoral programs and licensing exams. Same rights, same office, same activation ritual.

    For both readers

    Do the handoff audit tonight. List every school-related task an adult currently performs for the student — captures the assignments, watches the portal, emails the teachers, wakes them up. Each item gets a transfer date before the college cliff, and this year's items get the Chapter 9 treatment: built together, run by the student, reviewed weekly. A parent's success metric shifts across this chapter from "assignments turned in" to "systems running without me."

    Check yourself: 'My daughter's college grades are collapsing, so I called the dean's office and her professors to get her accommodations set up. They refused to even talk to me. Isn't this discrimination?' What does this chapter say? Click to check.

    It's the college pathway working as designed, not discrimination. Post-secondary disability law (ADA/504) makes the student the sole driver: she must self-identify to disability services, provide documentation, complete intake, and activate letters with professors — and FERPA bars the college from discussing her situation with a parent regardless of who pays tuition. The useful parental moves are all indirect: make sure she has the documentation (the evaluation report, meeting the office's recency rules), point her at the disability services office and this chapter's checklist, offer to rehearse her mechanism → pattern → ask, and support the daily-systems layer (Chapters 9-10) that accommodations don't cover. If the collapse is bigger than logistics — sleep gone, mood flat, isolation (Chapter 6's tells) — the right door is the campus counseling center, and she opens that one too. The hardest sentence in this chapter belongs here: at college, help that isn't invited isn't help the system can accept.

    What to Carry Out of This Chapter¶

    For the student:

    • Study actively or don't bother: retrieval with feedback beats rereading everywhere, and doubly so for your brain. Let apps that fire run the schedule.
    • Accommodations are the ramp, not a head start — and from college onward, nobody installs the ramp unless you ask. Register before the semester, activate every term, rehearse the twenty-second ask.
    • Choose majors and programs by interest-that-survived-boredom and by assessment shape. Structure is something you can pick on purpose.

    For the parent on the sidelines:

    • Use the machinery: written requests, the evaluation report, specific mechanism-linked asks, everything in writing, review dates on the calendar. "She's passing" is not a refusal that has to stand.
    • Your job has a transfer schedule: heavy lifting in elementary, co-building in middle, coaching in high school, invitation-only in college. Every task you still own at graduation is one the cliff will collect.
    • Homework wars are usually system gaps — capture, launchpad, submission ritual — not effort gaps. Build the missing link together before concluding anything about character.

    Chapter 13 runs the same playbook on the decades after school: choosing work that fits, workplace accommodations, and the disclosure decision that college never made you practice.
