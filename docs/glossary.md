# Glossary of Terms

Every term below is defined the way this book uses it, in plain language, in one flat alphabetical list. The terms come from the [learning graph](learning-graph/index.md)'s 300 concepts.

#### 504 Plan

A U.S. legal document, under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, that provides accommodations for a student with a disability, including ADHD, without requiring specialized instruction. A 504 Plan is generally used when a student can access the standard curriculum with adjustments like extra time or reduced distractions.

**Example:** A student's 504 Plan grants her extended time on tests and permission to take short movement breaks during long class periods.

See also: IEP, Requesting Accommodations

#### Academic Transitions

Points of change in a person's schooling, such as moving from elementary to middle school or high school to college, that shift the level of structure and independence required. Each academic transition tends to place new demands on executive function that can expose or worsen ADHD-related struggles.

**Example:** A student who did fine in a small elementary school with one teacher struggles the moment middle school hands her six different teachers and six different sets of expectations.

See also: Transition To College, ADHD In Adolescence

#### Accountability Partners

A person who checks in on progress toward a goal or task, providing external structure and motivation that can be harder to generate internally with ADHD. Accountability partners work best when check-ins are specific and scheduled, rather than left open-ended.

**Example:** A woman texts a friend "starting my taxes now" every Sunday afternoon, just so someone else knows and will ask how it went.

See also: Body Doubling, ADHD Coaching

#### Active Recall

A study technique that involves testing oneself on material rather than simply reviewing it, which strengthens memory more effectively than passive rereading. Active recall is especially useful for ADHD because it keeps the brain actively engaged rather than allowing attention to drift during passive review.

**Example:** Closing the textbook and writing down everything remembered from a chapter before checking it against the original.

See also: Study Strategies, Spaced Repetition

#### ADA Basics

An overview of the Americans with Disabilities Act, the U.S. federal law that prohibits discrimination against people with disabilities, including ADHD when it substantially limits a major life activity, and requires reasonable workplace accommodations. Understanding these basics is the foundation for requesting formal accommodations at work.

See also: Workplace Accommodations, Workplace Disclosure

#### ADD Vs ADHD Terminology

The distinction between the older term "ADD" (attention-deficit disorder), used before 1994, and the current term "ADHD," which now covers inattentive, hyperactive-impulsive, and combined presentations under one diagnosis. Many adults were diagnosed as children under the old term and still use it out of habit.

See also: DSM-5 Definition Of ADHD, Inattentive Presentation

#### ADHD

A neurodevelopmental disorder marked by persistent patterns of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity that begin in childhood and interfere with functioning across multiple settings such as school, work, and relationships. ADHD is well-documented in decades of research and is neither a character flaw nor a modern invention.

**Example:** A person with ADHD may excel at creative problem-solving yet struggle to pay a bill on time, because both stem from the same underlying differences in attention and regulation.

#### ADHD Across Cultures

The recognition that ADHD occurs in similar proportions across countries and cultures, though diagnosis rates, awareness, and stigma vary widely based on local healthcare access and cultural attitudes. This global consistency argues against the idea that ADHD is a product of any one culture or country.

See also: ADHD Prevalence, ADHD Stigma

#### ADHD And Dating

The particular considerations that come up when dating with ADHD, such as when and how to mention the diagnosis, and how traits like time blindness or impulsivity might show up early in a relationship. Being upfront about practical patterns, like a tendency to run late, can prevent early misunderstandings from being read as a lack of interest.

**Example:** A man mentions early on that he sometimes loses track of time, so a new date won't assume being fifteen minutes late means he doesn't care.

See also: Telling A New Partner, Partner Communication

#### ADHD And Intelligence

The established finding that ADHD has no consistent relationship with IQ — people with ADHD span the full range of intelligence, and the condition affects how consistently someone can apply their abilities, not how much ability they have.

**Example:** A gifted student with ADHD may test in the top percentile yet still forget homework and fail classes.

#### ADHD And Memory

The relationship between ADHD and difficulties in working memory and prospective memory, distinct from the long-term memory systems used for facts and events, which are generally unaffected. People with ADHD often remember experiences vividly but struggle to reliably retrieve intentions like appointments or errands.

**Example:** A woman remembers every detail of a vacation from ten years ago but can't recall whether she took her medication this morning.

See also: Working Memory, Prospective Memory

#### ADHD As Regulation Difference

A way of describing ADHD that centers on difficulty regulating attention, activity level, and emotion, rather than framing it as simply "too much" or "too little" of anything. This framing matters because it points toward strategies that support regulation instead of demanding more effort.

See also: ADHD, Emotional Dysregulation

#### ADHD Brain Maturation Lag

Research findings that some brain regions, particularly areas supporting executive function, reach peak maturity a few years later in children with ADHD compared to their peers, though they follow a similar developmental path. This delay, not a permanent deficit, helps explain why some symptoms ease as the brain continues to mature into the mid-twenties.

**Example:** A parent notices their 16-year-old with ADHD manages impulse control more like a 13-year-old, and takes comfort in knowing that gap tends to narrow with time.

See also: Brain Development, Prefrontal Cortex

#### ADHD Burnout

A state of deep mental and physical exhaustion that results from the sustained extra effort many people with ADHD expend to keep up with daily demands. Burnout can look like a sudden collapse of previously reliable coping strategies rather than a single dramatic event.

**Example:** A woman who has held everything together for years — job, kids, house — suddenly cannot get out of bed some mornings, her usual systems no longer enough.

See also: High Achievement Masking, Caregiver Burnout

#### ADHD Coaching

A supportive service in which a trained coach helps a person with ADHD set goals, build systems, and stay accountable for daily tasks and long-term projects. Coaching differs from therapy by focusing on practical strategy and follow-through rather than processing emotions or diagnosing conditions.

**Example:** A coach meets with a client every week not to talk about feelings, but to help her build a workable filing system for her freelance invoices.

See also: Accountability Partners, Body Doubling

#### ADHD Community

The broader network of people with ADHD who connect, in person or online, to share experience, information, and mutual support. Finding an ADHD community can be especially meaningful for those diagnosed later in life who spent years feeling alone in their struggles.

**Example:** A man who felt alone with his diagnosis for years finally finds an online group where people swap the exact same stories about lost keys and missed deadlines.

See also: Support Groups, Online ADHD Culture

#### ADHD In Adolescence

The presentation and impact of ADHD in the teenage years, when rising academic demands, increasing independence, and social pressures often expose difficulties that were previously managed with more support. Hyperactivity frequently becomes less visible in adolescence even as inattention and organizational struggles intensify.

**Example:** A once-easygoing eighth grader starts missing every assignment deadline as classes multiply and no one is tracking his planner for him anymore.

See also: Academic Transitions, ADHD In Childhood

#### ADHD In Adulthood

The presentation and impact of ADHD after the school years, when symptoms shift from classroom behavior toward challenges in work performance, relationships, and household management. Adult ADHD is now recognized as a lifelong condition for most people diagnosed in childhood, not something typically outgrown.

**Example:** A 35-year-old realizes the "disorganized" label from her twenties never actually went away — it just moved from missed classes to missed work deadlines.

See also: Late Diagnosis, ADHD Brain Maturation Lag

#### ADHD In Childhood

The presentation and impact of ADHD in school-age children, when symptoms most often first draw attention through behavior at school, difficulty with routines, and social friction with peers. Early identification in childhood can open the door to support that shapes a person's entire school experience.

**Example:** A second grader is sent to the principal's office weekly for talking out of turn and leaving his seat, long before anyone considers ADHD as the reason.

See also: DSM-5 Definition Of ADHD, School Accommodations

#### ADHD In College Years

The presentation and impact of ADHD once a young adult leaves the structure of high school for the independence of college, where reduced oversight often reveals executive function gaps that were previously covered by parents or teachers. This is a common point at which ADHD is first diagnosed or first becomes seriously disruptive.

**Example:** A freshman who breezed through high school with parental reminders suddenly can't keep up once she's the only one managing her own schedule.

See also: Transition To College, College Accommodations

#### ADHD In Girls

The presentation of ADHD in girls, which more often involves inattentive symptoms, internalized distress, and social masking rather than the visible hyperactivity that prompts many boys to be referred for evaluation. Because diagnostic tools were originally built around the more visible male presentation, girls are frequently overlooked.

**Example:** A quiet girl who stares out the classroom window is labeled a daydreamer for years, while a loud classmate with the same condition gets referred for testing in first grade.

See also: Inattentive Presentation, Gender Diagnosis Gap

#### ADHD In Older Adults

The presentation and impact of ADHD in later life, an understudied area where symptoms can be mistaken for normal aging, stress, or early cognitive decline. Some older adults are diagnosed only after a grandchild's evaluation prompts them to recognize lifelong patterns in themselves.

**Example:** A 68-year-old assumes his growing forgetfulness is just aging, until his granddaughter's ADHD diagnosis makes him recognize the same patterns he's had his whole life.

See also: Late Diagnosis, Reframing Your Past

#### ADHD In Women

The presentation and lived experience of ADHD in adult women, frequently shaped by years of masking, high achievement covering internal struggle, and symptoms that fluctuate with hormonal changes across the menstrual cycle and life stages. Many women are diagnosed only after a child's evaluation prompts them to recognize the same patterns in themselves.

**Example:** A woman spends her twenties being told she's "just anxious," never hearing anyone mention ADHD until her symptoms worsen in her thirties.

See also: Late Diagnosis In Women, Hormones And ADHD

#### ADHD Misinformation

Inaccurate claims about ADHD spread through social media, casual conversation, or outdated sources, ranging from denial that ADHD exists to unproven cures. Evaluating claims against research evidence helps separate credible information from misinformation.

See also: Evaluating Treatment Claims, Evidence-Based Information

#### ADHD Myths

Widely believed but inaccurate claims about ADHD, such as the idea that it results from bad parenting, too much sugar, or too much screen time. These myths persist despite being contradicted by decades of research and often add unnecessary shame.

See also: Laziness Myth, ADHD Misinformation

#### ADHD Prevalence

The proportion of a population that has ADHD at a given time, estimated at roughly 5-7 percent of children and 2.5-4 percent of adults worldwide. Prevalence estimates vary by country and diagnostic method, which matters when comparing rates across cultures.

#### ADHD Rating Scales

Standardized questionnaires that ask about the frequency and severity of ADHD-related behaviors, completed by the patient, a parent, or a teacher, and scored against normative data. Rating scales support a diagnosis but are not sufficient on their own without a full clinical evaluation.

See also: Self-Report Questionnaires, Diagnostic Evaluation

#### ADHD Stigma

The negative judgment, stereotyping, or discrimination directed at people with ADHD, often rooted in the myths that it reflects laziness or poor character. Stigma can delay diagnosis and discourage people from seeking treatment or accommodations.

**Example:** A coworker jokes that ADHD is "just an excuse," and the person who actually has it stays quiet rather than explain a diagnosis they worked hard to get.

See also: ADHD Myths, Shame And ADHD

#### ADHD Strengths

Traits and capacities sometimes associated with ADHD that can function as genuine advantages in the right context, such as creative thinking, high energy, or the ability to hyperfocus on an area of passion. Naming these strengths does not deny the real difficulties of ADHD — it reflects that the same brain differences can cut both ways depending on the situation.

**Example:** A man's manager singles him out for his ability to generate ten creative solutions in a brainstorm that stumps everyone else on the team.

See also: Creativity And ADHD, Hyperfocus

#### ADHD-Friendly Jobs

Roles or work environments that tend to align well with common ADHD traits, such as jobs offering variety, movement, clear short-term deadlines, or built-in external structure. No single job suits everyone with ADHD, since interests and strengths vary widely from person to person.

**Example:** A man who struggled in a job requiring hours of solitary paperwork thrives instead as a paramedic, where every shift brings something new.

See also: Career Fit, Interest-Based Nervous System

#### Adult Child Autonomy

The principle that an adult with ADHD retains the right to make their own decisions and manage their own life, even when family members could technically step in and manage things for them. Respecting this autonomy is central to a healthy relationship between an adult with ADHD and their family, even when it means watching them learn from a mistake.

**Example:** A father bites his tongue when his 26-year-old daughter forgets to renew her car registration, letting her handle the late fee herself.

See also: Supporting Without Parenting, Self-Advocacy

#### Adult Diagnosis Journey

The process, often spanning years, by which an adult comes to recognize lifelong patterns as ADHD, seeks an evaluation, and receives a formal diagnosis. This journey frequently begins when a child's diagnosis, a life transition, or a breakdown in compensation strategies prompts the adult to look closer at their own history.

**Example:** It takes a man eighteen months from his first "maybe I have ADHD" thought to an actual diagnosis, working through waitlists, forms, and a good deal of self-doubt along the way.

See also: Late Diagnosis, Reframing Your Past

#### Age Of Onset Criterion

The DSM-5 requirement that several ADHD symptoms must have been present before age 12, distinguishing ADHD from attention problems that begin later due to other causes. This criterion is why a thorough childhood history is essential even when evaluating an adult.

See also: Childhood History Requirement, DSM-5 Definition Of ADHD

#### Amphetamine Medications

A stimulant medication, sold under brand names such as Adderall and Vyvanse, that increases dopamine and norepinephrine availability in the brain and is one of the two main types of stimulant used to treat ADHD. Some people respond better to amphetamine-based stimulants than to methylphenidate-based ones, or vice versa, for reasons not fully understood.

See also: Stimulant Medications, Titration

#### Anxiety Disorders

Conditions marked by excessive, persistent worry or fear that interferes with daily life, frequently co-occurring with ADHD. Anxiety in ADHD can arise on its own or develop partly in response to years of struggling with unmanaged symptoms.

**Example:** A woman with ADHD develops a habit of triple-checking every email before sending, terrified she missed something important.

See also: Comorbidity, Internalized Symptoms

#### Appetite And Sleep Effects

Two of the most common side effects of stimulant medications: reduced appetite during the medication's active hours and difficulty falling asleep if a dose is taken too late in the day. These effects often can be managed by adjusting timing, dose, or with meal and sleep planning.

**Example:** A teenager skips lunch without noticing while on his medication, then can't fall asleep if he takes a dose too late in the afternoon.

See also: Side Effects, Medication Onset And Duration

#### Arousal Regulation

The ability to keep the brain's level of alertness matched to what a situation requires — awake enough to engage, calm enough to focus. In ADHD, arousal can run too low for boring tasks or too high under stress, contributing to both underactivation and restlessness.

**Example:** A man paces around the kitchen while on a work call, not because he's anxious, but because moving is the only way he can keep his mind on the conversation.

See also: Norepinephrine, Internal Restlessness

#### Atomoxetine

A non-stimulant medication, sold under the brand name Strattera, that increases norepinephrine availability in the brain and is approved for treating ADHD in children, adolescents, and adults. It is not a controlled substance and typically takes several weeks to reach its full effect.

See also: Non-Stimulant Medications, Controlled Substance Rules

#### Attention

The mental process of selecting, focusing on, and sustaining awareness of specific information while filtering out competing input. In ADHD, attention is not absent but unevenly allocated — hard to direct on demand, easy to lock onto what feels interesting.

**Example:** Someone with ADHD can read a tax form for two minutes before their mind drifts, then read a novel for three hours without noticing time pass.

#### Attention Regulation

The capacity to direct attention toward a chosen task, sustain it there, and shift it when the situation calls for a change. ADHD involves a specific difficulty regulating attention rather than a general lack of it.

**Example:** A student can regulate attention well during a gripping lecture but struggles to keep it on a repetitive worksheet.

See also: Attention, Executive Function

#### Autism And ADHD

The frequent co-occurrence of autism spectrum conditions and ADHD, which share some overlapping traits, such as difficulty with transitions, but also have distinct features, such as autism's differences in social communication. Recognizing both conditions when they co-occur allows support to address each set of needs rather than assuming one explains everything.

**Example:** A boy struggles with sudden schedule changes like an autistic child would, but also can't sit still or stop interrupting like a child with ADHD — because he has both.

See also: Comorbidity, Differential Diagnosis

#### Behavioral Parent Training

A structured program that teaches parents specific techniques for managing a child's ADHD-related behavior, such as consistent praise, clear expectations, and effective consequences. This approach is recommended as a first-line treatment for young children with ADHD, often before or alongside medication.

**Example:** A parent learns in a training program to praise her son immediately when he starts his homework, instead of only commenting when he fails to.

See also: Behavioral Therapy, Psychoeducation

#### Behavioral Therapy

A treatment approach that focuses on changing specific behaviors through structured techniques like reinforcement, skill-building, and environmental adjustment, rather than medication. Behavioral therapy is especially well studied for children with ADHD and their parents.

See also: Behavioral Parent Training, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy

#### Bipolar Disorder Distinction

The clinical process of telling ADHD apart from bipolar disorder, since both can involve impulsivity and rapid shifts in mood or energy, but bipolar disorder involves distinct episodes of mania or depression that ADHD does not. Getting this distinction right matters because the two conditions call for very different treatment approaches.

**Example:** A clinician carefully distinguishes a patient's constant, baseline impulsivity from the distinct manic episode that shows up only once every few years, since the two point to different diagnoses.

See also: Differential Diagnosis, Emotional Dysregulation

#### Body Doubling

The practice of completing a task while another person is present, in person or virtually, not necessarily helping directly but providing quiet accountability and shared focus. Many people with ADHD find that simply having someone else nearby makes starting and sustaining a task noticeably easier.

**Example:** Two friends on a video call, each silently working through their own separate to-do lists.

See also: Accountability Partners, Task Initiation

#### Boredom Intolerance

A strong aversion to tasks that feel understimulating, often experienced as physical discomfort rather than simple disinterest. Boredom intolerance helps explain why some people with ADHD seek out risk, novelty, or last-minute pressure to make a task feel engaging enough to sustain.

**Example:** A teenager abandons a puzzle the moment it stops feeling new, even though she was excited about it twenty minutes earlier.

See also: Delay Aversion, Interest-Based Nervous System

#### Brain Development

The process by which the brain grows, forms connections, and matures from before birth through early adulthood. In ADHD, this process follows a similar path but on a delayed timeline in some regions.

See also: ADHD Brain Maturation Lag, Prefrontal Cortex

#### Brain Networks

Groups of brain regions that activate together to support functions like focus, rest, and self-referential thought. ADHD research points to atypical coordination between these networks rather than damage to any single area.

See also: Default Mode Network, Prefrontal Cortex

#### Building A Care Team

The process of assembling the group of professionals and personal supports involved in managing ADHD, which might include a prescriber, therapist, coach, and family members. A well-coordinated care team communicates with each other, which helps keep a multimodal treatment plan consistent.

See also: Multimodal Treatment, Integrated Treatment Planning

#### Burnout Prevention At Work

Strategies for avoiding the cumulative exhaustion that can result from the extra effort many people with ADHD expend to keep pace with workplace demands, such as building in recovery time and being realistic about workload. Recognizing early signs of burnout, rather than pushing through until a full collapse, is central to prevention.

**Example:** A woman starts blocking one meeting-free afternoon a week specifically to recover, instead of waiting until she's too exhausted to keep going.

See also: ADHD Burnout, Caregiver Burnout

#### Capture Systems

A consistent method for immediately recording tasks, ideas, or reminders as soon as they come to mind, so they are not lost to a lapse in working memory. A reliable capture system only works if it is used in the moment, since the whole point is not depending on memory to store the thought until later.

**Example:** A woman keeps a single notes app open all day and jots down every stray thought or task the second it crosses her mind, instead of trying to hold onto it.

See also: Externalizing Memory, To-Do Lists That Work

#### Cardiovascular Considerations

The need to monitor heart rate and blood pressure when starting or adjusting stimulant medication, since stimulants can modestly raise both. Prescribers typically review personal and family cardiac history before starting a stimulant and monitor periodically afterward.

See also: Side Effects, Talking To Your Prescriber

#### Career Fit

The degree to which a job's structure, pace, and tasks align well with a person's individual strengths and challenges, which for someone with ADHD can mean seeking roles with variety, autonomy, or built-in accountability rather than long stretches of repetitive, unsupervised work. Finding good career fit is not about avoiding all challenge, but about avoiding a mismatch that constantly works against one's brain.

**Example:** A woman leaves a quiet desk job that left her drained and takes a role that has her moving between sites all day, and finds she's far more engaged.

See also: ADHD-Friendly Jobs, Transition To Work

#### Caregiver Burnout

A state of physical and emotional exhaustion in a caregiver, resulting from prolonged, unaddressed caregiver strain without enough relief or support. Left unaddressed, caregiver burnout can reduce a family's capacity to support the person with ADHD, making prevention a benefit to the whole household, not just the caregiver.

**Example:** After years of managing her husband's schedule on top of her own, a wife finally reaches a point where she has nothing left to give, even to things she used to enjoy.

See also: Caregiver Strain, Respite And Boundaries

#### Caregiver Role

The position of a family member — parent, partner, sibling, or adult child — who provides ongoing support to someone with ADHD, distinct from a clinical or professional role. This book addresses caregivers as a primary audience alongside the person with ADHD, since ADHD affects the people around a person as well.

**Example:** A sister helps her adult brother with ADHD set up a bill-paying system, a support role distinct from any therapist or doctor on his care team.

See also: Supporting Without Parenting, Caregiver Strain

#### Caregiver Self-Care

Deliberate practices a caregiver uses to maintain their own physical and emotional well-being while supporting a family member with ADHD. Caregiver self-care is not a luxury or a distraction from caregiving — it is what allows support to be sustained over the long term.

**Example:** A father schedules his own gym time every week, non-negotiable, because he knows he supports his son better when he isn't running on empty.

See also: Caregiver Strain, Respite And Boundaries

#### Caregiver Strain

The cumulative stress experienced by a family member who regularly manages tasks, reminders, or emotional support for someone with ADHD. Caregiver strain is a real and legitimate experience in its own right, not a sign of insufficient love or patience.

**Example:** A mother finds herself exhausted by the constant mental tracking of her son's medication, appointments, and school forms, even though she loves him deeply.

See also: Caregiver Role, Caregiver Burnout

#### Causes Of ADHD

The combination of genetic and biological factors, and to a lesser extent environmental factors, that lead to the brain differences underlying ADHD. No single cause explains every case; ADHD arises from many small contributing factors acting together.

See also: Genetics Of ADHD, Environmental Risk Factors

#### Childhood History Requirement

The diagnostic requirement that ADHD symptoms must have been present before age 12, even if they were not formally recognized at the time. In adult evaluations, clinicians rely on report cards, parent recollection, or old records to establish that this history exists.

See also: Age Of Onset Criterion, Collateral Information

#### Choosing A Major

The process of selecting a field of study in college, which for a student with ADHD can benefit from considering not just interest but also fit with executive function demands, such as heavy reading loads versus hands-on or project-based work. Matching a major to genuine interest can make the difference between struggling to stay engaged and thriving.

**Example:** A student passionate about art but weighed down by ADHD-related reading fatigue chooses a hands-on studio major over a heavy lecture-based one.

See also: Interest-Based Nervous System, Career Fit

#### Chronic Stress Effects

The cumulative impact of long-term stress on attention, memory, and emotional regulation, which can worsen ADHD symptoms and, in some cases, produce attention difficulties that mimic ADHD on their own. Distinguishing chronic stress from ADHD is part of a thorough evaluation.

**Example:** A woman going through a stressful divorce finds she suddenly can't focus at work at all, even though she was never formally diagnosed with ADHD.

See also: Trauma And ADHD, ADHD Burnout

#### Clinical Interview

A structured conversation between a clinician and a patient, and often a close family member, used to gather detailed history about symptoms, development, and daily functioning. The clinical interview is typically the core component of an ADHD evaluation.

See also: Diagnostic Evaluation, Collateral Information

#### Cognitive Behavioral Therapy

A talk therapy approach that helps a person identify and change unhelpful thought patterns and behaviors, adapted for ADHD to address challenges like procrastination, self-criticism, and disorganization. CBT for ADHD is often more skills-focused than traditional CBT, teaching concrete strategies alongside thought work.

**Example:** A therapist helps a man notice the thought "I always ruin everything" right after a missed deadline, and works with him to replace it with a more accurate one.

See also: Behavioral Therapy, Cognitive Reframing

#### Cognitive Flexibility

The ability to shift thinking between different concepts or adapt behavior when rules or circumstances change. People with ADHD often find it harder to switch gears smoothly, especially under time pressure.

**Example:** A plan changes at the last minute — the restaurant is closed — and it takes a person with ADHD several minutes to mentally regroup and pick somewhere else.

See also: Task Switching Difficulty, Executive Function

#### Cognitive Load

The total amount of mental effort being used at a given moment to process information and manage a task. People with ADHD often reach their cognitive load limit faster, especially with multiple competing demands, which increases mistakes and forgetfulness.

See also: Working Memory, Task Breakdown

#### Cognitive Reframing

The practice of consciously examining and adjusting an unhelpful thought, such as replacing "I always mess this up" with a more accurate and balanced view of the situation. Cognitive reframing is a core technique in cognitive behavioral therapy, useful for countering the shame that often builds around ADHD-related mistakes.

**Example:** Instead of "I'm so stupid for forgetting again," a woman practices telling herself "my system for this task needs an upgrade."

See also: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy, Self-Compassion

#### Collateral Information

Information about a person's symptoms and history gathered from someone other than the patient, such as a parent, sibling, partner, or old report card. Collateral information is especially valuable in adult evaluations because it helps confirm symptoms were present in childhood.

See also: Childhood History Requirement, Clinical Interview

#### College Accommodations

Adjustments provided to college students with ADHD, such as extended test time, note-taking support, or reduced-distraction testing environments, arranged through a college's disability services office. Unlike in K-12 education, college students are generally responsible for requesting and renewing their own accommodations each term.

**Example:** A student receives permission for extended time and a distraction-reduced room for exams, arranged each semester through her college's disability office.

See also: College Disability Services, Requesting Accommodations

#### College Disability Services

An office at a college or university responsible for reviewing documentation and approving academic accommodations for students with disabilities, including ADHD. Unlike K-12 schools, colleges do not automatically continue accommodations from an IEP or 504 Plan — students must register directly with this office.

**Example:** A freshman registers with her college's disability services office in her first week, submitting her old evaluation report to unlock testing accommodations.

See also: College Accommodations, Transition To College

#### Combined Presentation

A presentation of ADHD in which a person meets the criteria for both significant inattentive symptoms and significant hyperactive-impulsive symptoms. Combined presentation is the most commonly diagnosed form of ADHD in children.

See also: Inattentive Presentation, Hyperactive-Impulsive Type

#### Communication Foundations

The basic practices, such as clear requests and non-judgmental language, that support productive conversations between a person with ADHD and their family members. Strong communication foundations reduce the chance that ordinary ADHD-related friction escalates into recurring conflict.

**Example:** Instead of "you never listen," a wife tells her husband, "when the trash isn't taken out, I end up doing it and feeling unseen."

See also: Reminders Without Nagging, Parent-Child Conflict Cycles

#### Comorbidity

The presence of one or more additional conditions alongside ADHD, such as anxiety, depression, or a learning disability. Comorbidity is common rather than exceptional in ADHD — most people diagnosed have at least one co-occurring condition.

**Example:** A teenager is treated for anxiety for two years before a fuller evaluation reveals that undiagnosed ADHD was underneath it the whole time.

See also: Screening For Comorbidities, Differential Diagnosis

#### Compensation Strategies

Personal workarounds a person develops to manage ADHD symptoms without formal support, such as memorizing routines or relying on last-minute pressure to start tasks. Compensation strategies can work for years before breaking down under greater demands, which is a common trigger for adult diagnosis.

**Example:** A man has never used a calendar in his life — he just keeps everything close to a deadline, running on adrenaline, until the system finally breaks down in his thirties.

See also: Masking, Adult Diagnosis Journey

#### Continuous Performance Tests

Computer-based tasks that measure sustained attention and impulse control by asking a person to respond to certain stimuli and withhold responses to others over an extended period. These tests can support a clinical picture of ADHD but cannot diagnose it on their own, since performance can be affected by many factors.

See also: Neuropsychological Testing, Response Inhibition

#### Controlled Substance Rules

Government regulations that classify most stimulant ADHD medications as controlled substances, requiring specific prescribing rules such as limits on refills and, in some places, restrictions on telehealth prescribing. These rules exist to prevent misuse but can also create practical hurdles for patients who depend on consistent access.

See also: Medication Shortages, Telehealth Evaluations

#### Cost And Insurance

The financial and coverage considerations involved in getting evaluated and treated for ADHD, including evaluation fees, therapy costs, and medication copays. Cost is one of the most common practical barriers that delays people from seeking an ADHD evaluation.

See also: Finding A Clinician, Building A Care Team

#### Couples Counseling

Professional therapy for romantic partners aimed at improving communication and resolving recurring conflict, which can be particularly useful for couples navigating ADHD-related patterns like the partner-parent trap or unequal mental load. A therapist familiar with ADHD's effect on relationships can help a couple address the underlying pattern rather than just the surface argument.

**Example:** A couple sees a therapist together specifically to break the pattern where one partner manages everything and the other feels constantly criticized.

See also: Partner-Parent Trap, Partner Communication

#### Creativity And ADHD

The association, supported by some research, between ADHD and stronger performance on certain measures of creative or divergent thinking, potentially linked to a less filtered flow of ideas. This connection is one example of how ADHD traits can function as strengths in contexts that reward novel thinking.

**Example:** A woman's mind jumps between seemingly unrelated ideas during a meeting, landing on a genuinely novel product angle no one else considered.

See also: ADHD Strengths, Interest-Based Nervous System

#### Deadline Scaffolding

The practice of creating a series of earlier, smaller deadlines that lead up to a final one, rather than relying on a single distant due date. Deadline scaffolding counters time blindness by making progress visible well before the real deadline arrives.

**Example:** Instead of one deadline three weeks out, a student sets herself smaller due dates for the outline, the draft, and the final edit along the way.

See also: Time Estimation, Task Breakdown

#### Deadlines At Work

Externally imposed due dates for work tasks, which can serve as helpful structure for someone with ADHD but also create stress when time blindness leads to underestimating how much time a task will actually take. Breaking a work deadline into smaller internal milestones can help make progress visible well before the final date arrives.

**Example:** A woman consistently underestimates how long a project will take, so she now pads every deadline she gives herself by an extra day.

See also: Deadline Scaffolding, Time Estimation

#### Deciding About Medication

The personal decision-making process a person goes through when weighing whether to start, continue, adjust, or stop ADHD medication, made in partnership with a prescriber and informed by personal values and goals. This decision belongs to the patient and prescriber together — no one else can make it for them.

**Example:** A newly diagnosed adult weighs medication against therapy alone for weeks, eventually deciding to try a stimulant after talking it through with her prescriber.

See also: Talking To Your Prescriber, Multimodal Treatment

#### Decision Paralysis

Difficulty choosing between options, even minor ones, that leads to prolonged indecision or avoidance of the decision altogether. It often stems from working-memory limits that make it hard to hold and compare several options at once.

**Example:** Standing in front of a closet for ten minutes, unable to choose an outfit despite having plenty of options.

See also: Working Memory, Executive Function

#### Decluttering For ADHD

The process of reducing physical or digital clutter in a way that accounts for ADHD-related challenges with sorting, deciding, and follow-through, often by simplifying decisions and working in short sessions. Decluttering approaches built for ADHD tend to favor quick, low-decision systems over elaborate organizing schemes that are hard to maintain.

**Example:** Instead of trying to organize an entire closet in one exhausting weekend, a woman clears just one shelf at a time over several short sessions.

See also: Designated Places, System Maintenance

#### Default Mode Network

A brain network active during rest and mind-wandering that typically quiets down when a person focuses on a task. In ADHD, this network can stay more active during tasks, which researchers link to distractibility and mind wandering.

See also: Mind Wandering, Brain Networks

#### Defining Next Actions

The practice of identifying the single, concrete, immediately doable step required to move a task forward, rather than leaving a task described only by its larger goal. A defined next action removes the planning work that often stalls task initiation in ADHD.

**Example:** Replacing "clean the garage" with "put the five boxes by the door into the car."

See also: Task Breakdown, Task Initiation

#### Delay Aversion

A strong dislike of waiting for a reward or outcome, leading some people with ADHD to prefer a smaller immediate reward over a larger delayed one, or to avoid tasks with distant payoffs. Delay aversion can look like impatience but reflects a real difference in how waiting is experienced.

**Example:** Offered $20 now or $50 in a month, a teenager with ADHD takes the $20, even knowing it's the worse deal.

See also: Reward System, Time Blindness

#### Delayed Sleep Phase Syndrome

A circadian rhythm disorder in which a person's natural sleep and wake times are shifted significantly later than conventional schedules require, common among people with ADHD. This mismatch between a person's biological clock and society's schedule can look like poor discipline about bedtime when it is actually a physiological pattern.

**Example:** A teenager physically cannot fall asleep before 2 a.m. no matter how early he goes to bed, then can't wake for an 8 a.m. class.

See also: Sleep Disorders, Sleep And The ADHD Brain

#### Depression

A mood disorder marked by persistent sadness, loss of interest, or low energy that interferes with daily functioning, frequently co-occurring with ADHD. Depression can develop in response to the cumulative toll of unaddressed ADHD symptoms, especially after years of negative feedback.

**Example:** After years of missed deadlines and disappointed bosses, a man stops trying at all, convinced nothing he does will ever be good enough.

See also: Comorbidity, Years Of Negative Feedback

#### Designated Places

A fixed, consistent spot assigned to a specific item, such as keys or a wallet, so that putting the item away and finding it again does not depend on memory. Designated places directly address the common ADHD pattern of losing frequently used items.

**Example:** A single bowl by the front door that always holds keys and nothing else.

See also: Losing Things, Environment Design

#### Diagnostic Evaluation

The structured process a clinician uses to determine whether a person meets the criteria for ADHD, typically combining a clinical interview, rating scales, and a review of childhood history. A thorough evaluation also rules out other conditions that can look similar to ADHD.

See also: Clinical Interview, Differential Diagnosis

#### Differential Diagnosis

The clinical process of distinguishing ADHD from other conditions that can cause similar symptoms, such as anxiety, depression, or learning disabilities, to arrive at an accurate diagnosis. Because so many conditions overlap with ADHD symptoms, differential diagnosis is a key part of a thorough evaluation.

See also: Comorbidity, Diagnostic Evaluation

#### Digital Calendars

Electronic scheduling tools that store appointments and reminders outside of memory, often with notification features that prompt action at the right time. Digital calendars are most effective for ADHD when paired with reminders set well before an event, not just at the exact time it starts.

**Example:** A man adds every appointment to his phone the moment it's made, with a reminder set for the night before, not just the morning of.

See also: Reminder Apps, Externalizing Memory

#### Digital Therapeutics

Software-based interventions, including apps and video games, designed and studied specifically to improve ADHD symptoms such as attention, sometimes cleared by regulators as a treatment option. Digital therapeutics are a newer addition to the treatment landscape and generally used alongside, not instead of, established approaches.

**Example:** A child plays a clinically studied attention-training game for twenty minutes a day as part of a broader treatment plan alongside therapy.

See also: Multimodal Treatment, Evaluating Treatment Claims

#### Disclosure To Friends

The personal decision of whether, when, and how to tell friends about an ADHD diagnosis, which can range from a brief mention to a detailed explanation. There is no single right approach to disclosure — it depends on the relationship and what the person wants others to understand.

**Example:** A woman mentions her diagnosis casually to one close friend first, testing the water before deciding whether to tell the rest of her friend group.

See also: Explaining Your ADHD, Self-Advocacy

#### Disordered Eating

Irregular or harmful eating patterns, such as binge eating or skipping meals for long stretches, which occur at higher rates in people with ADHD due to factors like impulsivity, time blindness, and appetite changes from medication. Recognizing disordered eating as a distinct issue matters because it needs its own attention alongside ADHD treatment.

**Example:** A woman on a stimulant skips meals for hours without noticing, then binges late at night once the medication wears off and her appetite returns all at once.

See also: Comorbidity, Appetite And Sleep Effects

#### Distractibility

A heightened tendency to shift attention toward unrelated sounds, sights, or thoughts, away from the task at hand. Distractibility in ADHD reflects a brain that filters competing input less automatically, not a lack of interest in the task.

**Example:** A student sits down to read one paragraph and is three tabs deep in something unrelated before finishing the first sentence.

See also: Attention Regulation, Mind Wandering

#### Distraction Blockers

Tools or apps that limit access to distracting websites, notifications, or apps for a set period, supporting sustained attention by reducing the number of competing pulls on it. Distraction blockers reduce temptation but work best alongside other strategies like task breakdown, not as a stand-alone fix.

**Example:** A woman installs an app that locks her out of social media for two hours each morning so she can actually finish her work before checking her phone.

See also: Notification Management, Focus Blocks At Work

#### Dividing Household Labor

The process of assigning household tasks between family members in a way that accounts for ADHD-related strengths and challenges, rather than assuming an even split by default will work equally well for everyone. Matching tasks to what each person can reliably follow through on tends to reduce resentment more effectively than an arbitrary equal division.

**Example:** A couple assigns the partner with ADHD to tasks with natural variety, like errands and cooking, and the other partner to steady, routine tasks like bills.

See also: Reminders Without Nagging, Dividing Mental Load

#### Dividing Mental Load

The process of sharing the invisible planning, tracking, and remembering work of running a household or relationship, rather than leaving it disproportionately to one partner. In ADHD-affected relationships, mental load is often assumed by the partner without ADHD by default, which can build resentment if never explicitly discussed and rebalanced.

**Example:** A couple sits down and writes out every invisible task — school forms, gift buying, appointment scheduling — and splits the list instead of leaving it all to one person's memory.

See also: Dividing Household Labor, Partner-Parent Trap

#### Dopamine

A brain chemical that carries signals related to reward, motivation, and movement. ADHD is associated with differences in dopamine signaling, which helps explain both motivation difficulties and why stimulant medications, which increase dopamine availability, are effective.

See also: Reward System, How Stimulants Work

#### Dopamine Reuptake

The process by which dopamine is normally reabsorbed from the synapse back into the neuron that released it, ending its signaling effect. Many stimulant medications work by blocking this reuptake process, leaving more dopamine available in the synapse for longer.

See also: Dopamine, How Stimulants Work

#### Driving And ADHD

The research finding that ADHD, particularly untreated, is associated with a higher rate of driving errors, traffic violations, and accidents, largely due to distractibility and impulsivity behind the wheel. Treatment, including medication, has been shown to reduce this elevated driving risk.

**Example:** A man misses his exit twice in one week because his mind drifted mid-drive, prompting him to start using voice navigation for even familiar routes.

See also: Distractibility, Impulsivity

#### DSM-5 Definition Of ADHD

The current official criteria for diagnosing ADHD, published in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, requiring a set number of inattentive or hyperactive-impulsive symptoms present before age 12 and causing impairment in two or more settings. Clinicians use these criteria as the basis for a formal diagnosis.

See also: Symptom Criteria, Age Of Onset Criterion

#### Dyslexia

A specific learning disability that affects the ability to read accurately and fluently, despite adequate intelligence and instruction. Dyslexia commonly co-occurs with ADHD, and the combination can make school particularly demanding without the right support.

**Example:** A student reads far below her grade level despite obvious intelligence in conversation, a mismatch that points her teacher toward a dyslexia evaluation.

See also: Learning Disabilities, School Accommodations

#### Email And Message Triage

A system for regularly sorting incoming messages by urgency and required action, rather than reading and responding to them as they arrive throughout the day. Triage helps prevent the common ADHD pattern of an overflowing inbox becoming a source of chronic low-grade dread.

**Example:** Instead of answering emails as they arrive all day, a woman checks her inbox only at 10 a.m. and 3 p.m., sorting each message into "now," "later," or "delete."

See also: Notification Management, Prioritization Methods

#### Emotion Regulation Skills

Learnable techniques, such as naming a feeling or pausing before responding, that help manage the intensity and expression of emotional reactions. Because emotional dysregulation is common in ADHD, building these skills is often as important to daily functioning as managing attention itself.

**Example:** A man notices his jaw tightening before an argument and takes three slow breaths before responding, instead of firing back immediately.

See also: Emotional Dysregulation, Pausing Before Reacting

#### Emotional Dysregulation

Difficulty managing the intensity, duration, or expression of emotional responses, resulting in reactions that feel disproportionate to the situation that triggered them. Emotional dysregulation is not part of the formal ADHD diagnostic criteria but is reported by a large share of people with ADHD as one of its most disruptive features.

**Example:** A minor comment from a coworker triggering a wave of anger or hurt that lasts far longer than the comment itself would seem to warrant.

See also: Emotional Impulsivity, ADHD As Regulation Difference

#### Emotional Impulsivity

The tendency to express an emotional reaction immediately and intensely, without the usual pause between feeling and reaction. Emotional impulsivity is closely linked to the broader impulse-control difficulties in ADHD, applied specifically to emotional responses rather than actions.

**Example:** A man snaps at his wife the instant he feels criticized, the sharp reply out of his mouth before he's even fully registered what she said.

See also: Emotional Dysregulation, Pausing Before Reacting

#### Energy And Enthusiasm

The high level of drive, excitement, and engagement that many people with ADHD bring to activities or ideas that capture their genuine interest. This energy can be a real asset in the right setting, even though it can also tip into overcommitment or restlessness when not channeled well.

**Example:** A man throws himself into planning a friend's birthday with a level of excitement and detail that leaves the rest of the group amazed.

See also: ADHD Strengths, Interest-Based Nervous System

#### Entrepreneurship And ADHD

The relationship between ADHD traits, such as high energy, risk tolerance, and idea generation, and the demands of starting and running a business. Entrepreneurship can suit some ADHD strengths well, but it also requires deliberately building the structure and follow-through systems that a traditional employer would otherwise provide.

**Example:** A man channels his constant stream of new ideas into starting his own business, though he has to consciously build systems to follow through on the ones worth pursuing.

See also: ADHD Strengths, Career Fit

#### Environment Design

The deliberate arrangement of physical or digital surroundings to make desired behaviors easier and undesired behaviors harder, reducing reliance on memory or willpower. Environment design works with ADHD rather than against it, by lowering the effort required to do the right thing.

**Example:** Leaving running shoes by the front door the night before a planned morning walk.

See also: Designated Places, Distraction Blockers

#### Environmental Risk Factors

Non-genetic influences during pregnancy or early childhood, such as premature birth, low birth weight, or prenatal exposure to alcohol or tobacco, that are associated with a higher likelihood of ADHD. These factors contribute alongside genetics rather than causing ADHD on their own.

See also: Causes Of ADHD

#### Evaluating Treatment Claims

The skill of assessing whether a proposed ADHD treatment is backed by solid evidence, by checking for peer-reviewed research, realistic promises, and independence from a product being sold. This skill helps protect against the time and money lost to treatments with little real support behind them.

See also: Treatment Evidence Hierarchy, Supplement Claims

#### Evaluation Report

The written document a clinician produces after an ADHD evaluation, summarizing findings, the diagnosis, and often recommendations for treatment or accommodations. This report is frequently required to request accommodations at school or work.

See also: Diagnostic Evaluation, Requesting Accommodations

#### Evening Routines

A consistent sequence of steps followed before bed, often including preparing for the next day, which can reduce morning stress and support better sleep. Evening routines are a practical place to build in tomorrow's task preparation, when energy for planning may be higher than it will be the next morning.

**Example:** A man spends five minutes each night setting out tomorrow's keys, wallet, and badge by the door, so his rushed morning self doesn't have to hunt for them.

See also: Routines And Habits, Sleep Hygiene

#### Evidence-Based Information

Information about ADHD that is grounded in peer-reviewed research and clinical consensus rather than anecdote, marketing, or opinion. This book prioritizes evidence-based information over trends and testimonials.

See also: Treatment Evidence Hierarchy

#### Excessive Talking

Speaking more than a situation calls for, often driven by a stream of thoughts that feel urgent to share before they are lost. This is one of the hyperactive-impulsive symptoms listed in the DSM-5.

**Example:** A boy answers a teacher's question and keeps going for three more minutes of related thoughts before the teacher can move on.

See also: Interrupting, Hyperactive-Impulsive Type

#### Executive Function

The set of mental skills — including working memory, planning, self-monitoring, and impulse control — that let a person manage their own behavior toward a goal. ADHD is best understood as a disorder of executive function, not a lack of knowledge or intent.

See also: Working Memory, Response Inhibition

#### Exercise As Treatment

The use of regular physical activity as a supplementary strategy for managing ADHD symptoms, supported by research showing exercise can temporarily boost dopamine and norepinephrine activity and improve focus. Exercise is a useful addition to core treatments like medication and therapy, not a replacement for them.

**Example:** A man notices he can concentrate noticeably better on days he goes for a run before work than on days he skips it.

See also: Multimodal Treatment, Sleep Hygiene

#### Explaining Your ADHD

The skill of describing one's own ADHD and its practical effects to another person in clear, specific terms, rather than relying on labels alone. Explaining ADHD in terms of concrete needs, such as "I focus better with written instructions," tends to be more useful to listeners than a general description of the diagnosis.

**Example:** Instead of just saying "I have ADHD," a woman tells her new roommate, "I need visual reminders or I'll genuinely forget, it's not about caring."

See also: Self-Advocacy, Disclosure To Friends

#### Externalizing Memory

The strategy of moving information out of one's head and into an external system, such as a written list or an app, rather than relying on internal memory to hold onto it. Externalizing memory is one of the most effective and widely recommended strategies for managing ADHD-related forgetfulness.

**Example:** Keeping a single running note on a phone for everything that needs to be remembered, instead of trying to hold it all mentally.

See also: Capture Systems, Prospective Memory

#### Family Meetings

Regularly scheduled conversations among family members to discuss schedules, responsibilities, or concerns in a structured, calmer setting than an in-the-moment conflict allows. Family meetings give ADHD-related friction a predictable outlet, rather than letting it surface only during stressful moments.

**Example:** A family sets aside fifteen minutes every Sunday night to go over the week's schedule together, instead of surprises popping up mid-week.

See also: Repair After Conflict, Shared Family Calendars

#### Family Psychoeducation

The process of teaching an entire family accurate, practical information about ADHD, including how it affects the diagnosed member and how it affects the family system as a whole. Family psychoeducation helps replace blame and misunderstanding with a shared, accurate picture of what is actually happening.

**Example:** A family attends a session together where a clinician explains why their son forgets instructions so easily, replacing months of frustration with a shared understanding.

See also: Psychoeducation, Family System Impact

#### Family Support Plan

A document created collaboratively by the family members of a person with ADHD that lays out how they will support that person while respecting their autonomy, addressing communication, division of labor, and caregiver boundaries. The Family Support Plan is written by the caregivers as a counterpart to the Personal Owner's Manual written by the person with ADHD.

**Example:** A family writes out, together, who handles reminders, how they'll communicate about missed tasks, and what boundaries each caregiver needs, all in one shared document.

See also: Personal Owner's Manual, Supporting Without Parenting

#### Family System Impact

The way ADHD in one family member affects the dynamics, routines, and emotional climate of the whole household, not just the individual diagnosed. Recognizing this wider impact helps families address ADHD as a shared challenge rather than one person's private problem.

**Example:** When a father starts treatment for his ADHD, the whole household's morning routine gets calmer, not just his own day.

See also: Caregiver Role, Communication Foundations

#### Fidgeting

Small, repetitive movements such as tapping, shifting, or handling an object, often used consciously or unconsciously to manage restlessness or maintain focus. For some people with ADHD, fidgeting actually helps sustain attention rather than distract from it.

**Example:** Doodling in the margins of notes while still following every word of a meeting.

See also: Hyperactivity, Internal Restlessness

#### Finding A Clinician

The practical process of locating a qualified professional to evaluate or treat ADHD, which can involve checking insurance networks, waitlists, and specialty in adult versus childhood ADHD. Finding a clinician experienced with adult ADHD, and with women's presentations specifically, can significantly shape the quality of an evaluation.

See also: Who Can Diagnose ADHD, Cost And Insurance

#### Finding The Right Dose

The individualized process, guided by a prescriber, of identifying the medication dose that manages ADHD symptoms effectively while minimizing side effects. Because response to ADHD medication varies significantly between people, the right dose cannot be predicted from weight or age alone.

**Example:** A man tries three different doses over two months before landing on the one that helps him focus without making him feel flat or wired.

See also: Titration, Tracking Medication Response

#### Focus Blocks At Work

Scheduled, protected periods of uninterrupted time set aside for concentrated work on a single task, reducing the cost of task switching common in open workplaces. Focus blocks work best when paired with notification management, since even a brief interruption can derail sustained attention.

**Example:** A man blocks 9 to 11 a.m. on his calendar every day as "no meetings," using it as protected time to get his hardest task done first.

See also: Distraction Blockers, Task Switching Difficulty

#### Forgetfulness

A pattern of losing track of information, tasks, or plans shortly after they were learned or made, tied to weaknesses in working memory and prospective memory. Forgetfulness in ADHD is chronic and situation-spanning, not an occasional lapse.

**Example:** A man walks past the same reminder note on the fridge for three days without registering that it applies to him.

See also: Working Memory, Prospective Memory

#### Gender Diagnosis Gap

The documented disparity in ADHD diagnosis rates between boys and girls, and between men and women, driven by diagnostic criteria and clinical awareness shaped mostly around the more visible male presentation. This gap means many girls and women live for years without a name for what they are experiencing.

**Example:** In a single family, a hyperactive son is diagnosed at age seven while his quietly inattentive sister isn't diagnosed until she's thirty.

See also: ADHD In Girls, Media Portrayals Of ADHD

#### Genetics Of ADHD

The inherited component of ADHD, with twin and family studies estimating heritability at roughly 70 to 80 percent, making ADHD one of the most heritable conditions in psychiatry. Many parents of a child with ADHD recognize similar traits in themselves once they learn this.

**Example:** A parent seeking evaluation for their child may realize during the process that they have undiagnosed ADHD themselves.

See also: Heritability Research, Causes Of ADHD

#### Grad School With ADHD

The particular demands graduate school places on a student with ADHD, including long, self-directed projects like a thesis that require sustained motivation without the regular structure of coursework. Building in external deadlines and accountability, such as a regular advisor check-in, can help offset the lack of built-in structure.

**Example:** A graduate student schedules a standing weekly check-in with his advisor specifically because a thesis with no built-in deadlines is otherwise too easy to let slide.

See also: Task Initiation, Accountability Partners

#### Grief After Late Diagnosis

The sense of loss that can follow an adult ADHD diagnosis, connected to years lived without understanding, support, or accommodations that might have changed the course of school, work, or relationships. This grief is a normal and common response, not a sign that the diagnosis itself was somehow a mistake.

**Example:** A woman diagnosed at 42 mourns the career she might have had if someone had caught her ADHD in childhood instead of calling her "scattered."

See also: Late Diagnosis, Reframing Your Past

#### Guanfacine

A non-stimulant medication, sold under the brand name Intuniv in its extended-release form, originally developed to treat high blood pressure and now also approved to treat ADHD, particularly helpful for hyperactivity and emotional regulation. It is sometimes prescribed alongside a stimulant for added symptom coverage.

See also: Non-Stimulant Medications, Off-Label Options

#### Habit Stacking

The technique of attaching a new habit to an existing, already-automatic routine, using the established habit as a reliable cue. Habit stacking helps a new behavior become automatic faster, which is useful given how much repetition ADHD-related forgetfulness can require.

**Example:** Placing vitamins next to the coffee maker so taking them becomes attached to the automatic act of starting coffee each morning.

See also: Routines And Habits, Environment Design

#### Handling Feedback At Work

Strategies for receiving and processing workplace criticism in a way that separates useful information from an emotionally amplified reaction, relevant given how strongly some people with ADHD respond to perceived criticism. Pausing before responding to feedback, and revisiting it once the initial emotional charge has faded, often leads to a more accurate read of what was actually said.

**Example:** A man reads a critical performance review, sets it aside for an hour to let the sting fade, then reads it again to see what's actually useful in it.

See also: Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria, Pausing Before Reacting

#### Heritability Research

Studies, especially of twins and families, that estimate how much of the variation in ADHD across a population is explained by genetic factors, consistently finding a heritability of around 70 to 80 percent. This makes ADHD one of the more heritable conditions studied in psychiatry, on par with height.

See also: Genetics Of ADHD

#### High Achievement Masking

A pattern in which strong academic or professional performance conceals significant underlying struggle, exhaustion, or ADHD symptoms that the person compensates for at high personal cost. High achievers with ADHD are often disbelieved when they finally seek help, because their outward success seems to contradict the diagnosis.

**Example:** A straight-A student who studies until 3 a.m. every night just to keep pace with peers who need half the time.

See also: Masking, ADHD Burnout

#### History Of ADHD

The record of how medical and scientific understanding of ADHD has changed, from early 20th-century descriptions of restless children to today's recognition of a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition. Knowing this history helps explain why ADHD is sometimes mistaken for a recent trend, when it has been studied for over a century.

#### Homework Systems

Structured routines for tracking, starting, and completing homework assignments, designed to compensate for difficulties with prospective memory and task initiation. A homework system that includes a fixed time, place, and short first step tends to work better than relying on remembering to do homework "later."

**Example:** A family sets up a fixed spot and a fixed 4 p.m. start time for homework every day, instead of leaving it to be remembered "whenever."

See also: To-Do Lists That Work, Task Initiation

#### Hormones And ADHD

The influence of estrogen and other hormones on dopamine activity, which can cause ADHD symptoms to fluctuate across the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and menopause. This connection is an active area of research and one reason ADHD symptoms in women can feel inconsistent from week to week.

**Example:** A woman notices her focus and mood swing noticeably worse in the same week every month, long before she connects it to her cycle.

See also: Menstrual Cycle Effects, Menopause And ADHD

#### How Stimulants Work

The mechanism by which stimulant medications increase the amount of dopamine and norepinephrine available in the brain's synapses, improving the signaling involved in attention, impulse control, and motivation. This mechanism is well studied and explains why stimulants are effective for the specific brain chemistry differences seen in ADHD, not because of a sedative effect.

See also: Dopamine Reuptake, Stimulant Medications

#### Hyperactive-Impulsive Type

A presentation of ADHD marked mainly by excessive movement, restlessness, and impulsive behavior, with fewer significant attention difficulties. This presentation is often identified earlier because the behavior is more visible to teachers and parents.

See also: Hyperactivity, Impulsivity

#### Hyperactivity

A pattern of excessive movement, restlessness, or internal drive to be active that exceeds what a situation calls for. In adults it often shows up as an internal feeling of restlessness rather than visible fidgeting.

**Example:** A child who cannot stay seated through dinner, or an adult who feels compelled to tap a leg through every meeting.

See also: Internal Restlessness, Fidgeting

#### Hyperfocus

A state of deep, sustained absorption in an activity that a person finds especially interesting or stimulating, to the point that awareness of time, surroundings, or other obligations fades. Hyperfocus is a double-edged trait — it can produce remarkable output but also cause missed meals, appointments, or deadlines.

**Example:** Losing track of four hours while organizing a hobby collection, then missing a scheduled phone call entirely.

See also: Time Blindness, Interest-Based Nervous System

#### Identity After Diagnosis

The process of integrating an ADHD diagnosis into one's sense of self, which can involve both relief at finally having an explanation and adjustment to seeing familiar traits and struggles in a new light. This process takes time and often continues well beyond the initial diagnosis appointment.

**Example:** A man spends months rethinking his whole life story after diagnosis, realizing "forgetful" and "unreliable" were never really about who he was as a person.

See also: What A Diagnosis Means, Grief After Late Diagnosis

#### IEP

An Individualized Education Program, a legally binding U.S. document that outlines specific special education services and accommodations for a student whose disability, including ADHD in some cases, affects their ability to learn. An IEP is used when a student needs specialized instruction, not just accommodations, to access their education.

**Example:** A boy's IEP specifies that he receives small-group instruction for reading and extra time on written assignments, tailored to his specific needs.

See also: 504 Plan, School Accommodations

#### Immediate Vs Extended Release

The distinction between medication formulations that release their full dose quickly and wear off sooner, versus formulations engineered to release medication gradually over many hours. The choice between these formulations affects how often a dose needs to be taken and how smoothly its effects wear off.

See also: Methylphenidate, Medication Onset And Duration

#### Impairment Criterion

The DSM-5 requirement that ADHD symptoms must cause real difficulty functioning in two or more settings, such as home, school, or work, not just be present. Symptoms alone are not enough for a diagnosis — they must actually get in the way of daily life.

See also: DSM-5 Definition Of ADHD, Symptom Thresholds

#### Imposter Feelings

The persistent sense of not truly deserving one's accomplishments, often masking the fear that success has depended on effort or luck that cannot be sustained. Imposter feelings are common among high-achieving people with ADHD, who may credit their accomplishments to compensating hard rather than to genuine ability.

**Example:** A newly promoted woman is convinced her success came from luck and long hours covering for her ADHD, not from real skill.

See also: High Achievement Masking, Self-Esteem And ADHD

#### Impulsivity

Acting, speaking, or deciding before fully weighing the consequences, driven by a reduced ability to pause between urge and action. It is one of the three core symptom domains of ADHD.

**Example:** Blurting out an answer before a question is finished, or buying an item online the moment it catches the eye.

See also: Response Inhibition, Decision Paralysis

#### Inattention

Difficulty sustaining focus, following through on tasks, or attending to details, especially for activities that are not intrinsically engaging. It is one of the three core symptom domains of ADHD.

**Example:** Starting a work report, then finding an hour has passed spent reading unrelated articles.

#### Inattentive Presentation

A presentation of ADHD marked mainly by difficulty sustaining attention, organizing tasks, and following through, without significant hyperactivity or impulsivity. This presentation is more common in girls and women and is frequently missed because it looks like daydreaming rather than disruption.

See also: ADHD In Girls, Gender Diagnosis Gap

#### Independent Living Skills

The practical abilities needed to manage daily life on one's own, including cooking, cleaning, paying bills, and keeping appointments. ADHD can make independent living skills harder to establish and maintain, especially right after leaving a more structured household.

**Example:** A young man moves into his first apartment and discovers that keeping track of laundry, groceries, and rent all at once is far harder than any of them was alone.

See also: Money Management With ADHD, Executive Function

#### Integrated Treatment Planning

An approach to care that addresses ADHD and any co-occurring conditions together as one coordinated plan, rather than treating each in isolation. Integrated planning helps prevent one untreated condition from undoing progress made on another.

See also: Building A Care Team, Treatment Order Decisions

#### Interest-Based Nervous System

A description of ADHD motivation in which engagement is driven by interest, novelty, urgency, or challenge rather than by a task's importance or deadline. The term helps explain why a person with ADHD can focus intensely on a hobby but not on an equally important but less stimulating task.

**Example:** Spending hours absorbed in building a model but unable to start a five-minute form.

See also: Motivation And ADHD, Hyperfocus

#### Internal Restlessness

A subjective feeling of inner agitation or being unable to settle, often experienced by adults with ADHD in place of the visible physical hyperactivity seen in children. It can feel like a constant hum of mental or physical unease even while sitting still.

**Example:** A woman sits perfectly still in a meeting while her mind and body both feel like they're vibrating, desperate for it to end.

See also: Hyperactivity, Restlessness In Adults

#### Internalized Symptoms

ADHD symptoms that show up as inner experiences such as anxiety, self-criticism, or overwhelm rather than outwardly disruptive behavior. Internalized symptoms are more common in girls and women and often get treated as a separate mood problem instead of a sign of underlying ADHD.

**Example:** A girl never disrupts class, but privately spends every lesson consumed by anxious thoughts about the work she isn't following.

See also: ADHD In Girls, Comorbidity

#### Interrupting

Breaking into a conversation or activity before a natural pause, often because a thought or reply couldn't be held in mind long enough to wait. Interrupting in ADHD usually reflects weak response inhibition rather than poor manners.

**Example:** A woman finishes her sister's sentence for her, certain she already knows where it's going, and doesn't realize how often she does it until her sister points it out.

See also: Response Inhibition, Impulsivity

#### Intimacy And ADHD

The ways ADHD traits, such as distractibility, emotional dysregulation, or hyperfocus on other interests, can affect closeness and connection in a romantic relationship. Understanding these traits as brain-based patterns, rather than a lack of love or attention, can help both partners address them without assigning blame.

**Example:** A partner learns not to take it personally when her boyfriend's mind drifts mid-conversation, understanding it as distractibility rather than disinterest.

See also: Partner Communication, Emotional Dysregulation

#### Job Search With ADHD

The process of looking for employment in a way that accounts for ADHD-related challenges, such as breaking the search into small daily tasks and using external deadlines to counter procrastination on applications. Treating a job search like any other large project, with task breakdown and accountability, tends to work better than expecting motivation alone to carry it through.

**Example:** A woman treats her job search like a project with a daily task list — three applications a day — instead of leaving it to whenever she feels motivated.

See also: Task Breakdown, Career Fit

#### Journaling

The practice of regularly writing down thoughts, feelings, or events, which some people with ADHD use to externalize working memory, process emotions, or track patterns over time. Journaling works best for ADHD when kept brief and low-pressure, since an elaborate format is easy to abandon.

**Example:** A man jots down three lines each night about what went well and what didn't, using it to spot patterns in his own focus over time.

See also: Externalizing Memory, Emotion Regulation Skills

#### Late Diagnosis

Receiving an ADHD diagnosis in adulthood, often decades after symptoms began, typically because earlier signs were missed, masked, or attributed to other causes. Late diagnosis is especially common among women and people who compensated well enough to avoid detection.

**Example:** A 40-year-old accountant is diagnosed with ADHD only after her son's evaluation makes her realize her own "quirks" have a name.

See also: Adult Diagnosis Journey, Gender Diagnosis Gap

#### Late Diagnosis In Women

The specific and common pattern of women receiving an ADHD diagnosis well into adulthood, after years of masking, misattributed symptoms, and misdiagnosis with anxiety or depression alone. Naming this pattern helps women recognize it is not a personal failure to have been overlooked for so long.

**Example:** A 45-year-old mother cries in her diagnostic appointment, finally understanding why she always felt like she was working twice as hard as everyone else just to keep up.

See also: ADHD In Women, Masking

#### Laziness Myth

The false belief that people with ADHD fail to complete tasks because they lack effort or discipline. Research shows the real barrier is a difference in brain-based regulation of attention and motivation, not a shortage of willpower.

**Example:** A person who spends hours perfecting a hobby project but cannot start a simple chore is not lazy — their brain engages differently with each task.

See also: Willpower Myth

#### Learning Disabilities

Conditions that specifically affect the acquisition of skills like reading, writing, or math, distinct from ADHD but frequently co-occurring with it. Because ADHD affects attention and working memory, it can compound the impact of a learning disability on schoolwork.

**Example:** A boy with ADHD and a reading-based learning disability needs different support for each — one for staying on the page, one for decoding the words on it.

See also: Dyslexia, Comorbidity

#### Losing Things

A recurring difficulty keeping track of the physical location of belongings such as keys, phones, or paperwork. It is listed directly in the DSM-5 symptom criteria and often responds well to designated storage places.

**Example:** A teenager loses her third pair of headphones this semester, each time swearing she put them "right there" a moment ago.

See also: Designated Places, Forgetfulness

#### Managing Meetings

Strategies for staying engaged, tracking commitments, and following up effectively during work meetings, addressing common ADHD challenges like mind wandering and forgetting action items. Taking written notes on assigned tasks during a meeting, rather than trusting memory, is one of the most effective ways to manage this challenge.

**Example:** A woman writes down every action item assigned to her the moment it's said in a meeting, rather than trusting she'll remember it afterward.

See also: Note-Taking Systems, Working With A Manager

#### Masking

The conscious or unconscious effort to hide ADHD traits by over-preparing, mimicking peers, or suppressing visible symptoms in order to fit in or avoid judgment. Masking is common in girls and women and often delays diagnosis because the struggle underneath goes unseen.

**Example:** A woman rehearses small talk before every meeting and mimics her coworkers' habits so closely that no one at work suspects how much effort it costs her.

See also: Compensation Strategies, High Achievement Masking

#### Measuring Treatment Progress

The ongoing process of tracking whether an ADHD treatment plan is working, using tools like symptom rating scales, self-reports, and feedback from family or coworkers. Regularly measuring progress allows a care team to adjust the plan rather than assuming an initial choice will always be the right one.

See also: Tracking Medication Response, Building A Care Team

#### Media Portrayals Of ADHD

The way movies, television, and news coverage depict ADHD, which often exaggerates hyperactivity in young boys while ignoring quieter presentations in girls, women, and adults. These narrow portrayals contribute to underdiagnosis in groups that don't match the stereotype.

See also: Gender Diagnosis Gap, ADHD Misinformation

#### Medication And Comorbidities

The considerations a prescriber weighs when choosing ADHD medication for someone who also has another condition, such as anxiety or a tic disorder, since some medications can affect co-occurring conditions differently. This is one reason ADHD treatment planning benefits from full disclosure of a person's complete health history.

See also: Comorbidity, Integrated Treatment Planning

#### Medication Holidays

A planned, prescriber-approved pause in taking ADHD medication, sometimes used on weekends or school breaks to allow appetite to recover or to reassess ongoing need. Medication holidays are a clinical decision made with a prescriber, not something to start or stop without guidance.

**Example:** With her prescriber's guidance, a mother lets her son skip his stimulant on weekends so his appetite can catch up after a week of reduced eating.

See also: Deciding About Medication, Talking To Your Prescriber

#### Medication Onset And Duration

How quickly an ADHD medication begins working after it is taken and how long its effects last, which varies by medication type and formulation. Understanding onset and duration helps in planning a dose around the parts of the day that need the most support.

See also: Immediate Vs Extended Release, Titration

#### Medication Overview

A general summary of the medications used to treat ADHD, which fall into two main categories — stimulants and non-stimulants — each working through different mechanisms to improve attention and impulse control. Medication decisions are made individually with a prescriber, since response and side effects vary from person to person.

See also: Stimulant Medications, Non-Stimulant Medications

#### Medication Shortages

Periods during which certain ADHD medications become difficult to fill at pharmacies due to manufacturing or supply issues, which have affected stimulant medications repeatedly in recent years. Shortages can force temporary switches between medications or formulations, best navigated with a prescriber's guidance.

**Example:** A woman calls five pharmacies in one week trying to find her usual prescription in stock during a nationwide shortage.

See also: Controlled Substance Rules, Talking To Your Prescriber

#### Menopause And ADHD

The transition during which declining estrogen levels can intensify ADHD symptoms such as forgetfulness and difficulty focusing, sometimes leading to a new or first-time diagnosis in midlife. Because menopause and ADHD share overlapping symptoms, the two are sometimes confused for each other.

**Example:** A 52-year-old going through menopause struggles with forgetfulness so severe she wonders if it's early dementia, before learning it's a known interaction with her ADHD.

See also: Hormones And ADHD, Late Diagnosis In Women

#### Menstrual Cycle Effects

The tendency for ADHD symptoms, particularly focus and emotional regulation, to worsen in the days before menstruation when estrogen levels drop. Recognizing this pattern can help distinguish a predictable hormonal dip from a sign that a treatment plan has stopped working.

**Example:** A woman who manages her ADHD well most of the month finds her medication suddenly feels like it's "not working" for a few predictable days before her period.

See also: Hormones And ADHD

#### Methylphenidate

A stimulant medication, sold under brand names such as Ritalin and Concerta, that increases dopamine and norepinephrine availability in the brain and is one of the two main types of stimulant used to treat ADHD. It is available in both immediate-release and extended-release forms.

See also: Stimulant Medications, Immediate Vs Extended Release

#### Mind Wandering

The spontaneous drifting of thoughts away from a current task toward unrelated topics, memories, or plans. Mind wandering happens to everyone, but it occurs more often and is harder to redirect in ADHD.

**Example:** A man realizes, three paragraphs into a book, that he has no idea what he just read because his thoughts had drifted to dinner plans.

See also: Default Mode Network, Distractibility

#### Mindfulness And Meditation

Practices that train sustained, non-judgmental attention to the present moment, which some studies show can modestly improve attention and emotional regulation in people with ADHD. Mindfulness works best as a complement to other treatments, since it requires the same sustained attention that ADHD makes difficult to build in the first place.

**Example:** A man practices five minutes of breathing exercises each morning and notices he catches his mind wandering a little faster than before.

See also: Emotion Regulation Skills, Multimodal Treatment

#### Misdiagnosis

Receiving an incorrect diagnosis, such as being told a person has only anxiety or depression when undiagnosed ADHD is also present, or vice versa. Misdiagnosis is common because ADHD symptoms overlap heavily with several other conditions.

See also: Differential Diagnosis, Comorbidity

#### Momentum And Activation

The observation that, in ADHD, the hardest part of a task is often the initial activation, while continuing once started tends to become easier as momentum builds. Understanding this pattern shifts the goal from "finish the whole task" to "just begin," which is often enough to carry the rest forward.

**Example:** A man dreads starting the dishes but, once he's washed the first plate, finds it's suddenly easy to keep going until the sink is empty.

See also: Task Initiation, Starting Rituals

#### Money Management With ADHD

The particular challenges ADHD can create around budgeting, bill paying, and impulse spending, driven by working-memory limits, time blindness, and a reward system drawn to immediate over delayed benefits. Many adults with ADHD build external systems, like automated payments, specifically to work around these challenges.

**Example:** A woman sets up autopay for every bill she has, not because she's forgetful about money in general, but because she knows she'll forget the due dates otherwise.

See also: Impulsivity, Externalizing Memory

#### Morning Routines

A consistent sequence of steps followed at the start of each day, reducing the number of decisions and task initiations required during a time that is often mentally taxing. A written or visual version of a morning routine can help it stick on days when memory alone would not be enough.

**Example:** A teenager lays out her clothes, backpack, and lunch the night before, so her groggy morning self has one less decision to make.

See also: Routines And Habits, Visual Reminders

#### Motivation And ADHD

The way ADHD affects the internal drive to begin and continue tasks, especially ones that are not immediately rewarding or interesting. Motivation problems in ADHD are not a matter of caring less — they reflect how the brain's reward system responds to different kinds of tasks.

**Example:** A teenager can't make herself start a school essay but stays up until 2 a.m. voluntarily finishing a side project no one asked her to do.

See also: Interest-Based Nervous System, Reward System

#### Multimodal Treatment

An approach to managing ADHD that combines more than one type of intervention, such as medication, behavioral therapy, coaching, and lifestyle changes, rather than relying on a single method alone. Research generally supports multimodal treatment as more effective than any single approach used in isolation for most people.

See also: Medication Overview, Behavioral Therapy

#### Neurodevelopmental Disorder

A condition that arises from differences in how the brain develops, typically appearing in childhood and affecting functioning across the lifespan. ADHD is classified as a neurodevelopmental disorder, alongside conditions like autism.

#### Neurodiversity

The idea that variation in brain function, including ADHD and autism, is a natural part of human diversity rather than something to be uniformly corrected. The framework does not deny that ADHD causes real difficulty, but it resists treating every difference as a defect.

See also: ADHD, Neurotypical Brain

#### Neurofeedback Evidence

Research on neurofeedback, a technique that trains a person to alter their own brainwave patterns using real-time feedback, showing mixed and generally modest results for treating ADHD compared to established treatments. Because the evidence remains limited, neurofeedback is best considered experimental rather than a proven first-choice treatment.

See also: Treatment Evidence Hierarchy, Evaluating Treatment Claims

#### Neuroimaging Findings

Results from brain-scanning studies, such as MRI, that show group-level differences in brain structure, activity, and connectivity between people with and without ADHD. No single scan can diagnose ADHD in an individual — these findings describe patterns across groups, not a personal test.

See also: Brain Networks, Diagnostic Evaluation

#### Neuropsychological Testing

A set of standardized tasks that measure cognitive functions such as attention, memory, and processing speed, sometimes used alongside a clinical evaluation to build a fuller picture of strengths and weaknesses. This testing can help distinguish ADHD from other conditions but is not required for every diagnosis.

See also: Continuous Performance Tests, Differential Diagnosis

#### Neurotypical Brain

A brain whose development and functioning fall within the range considered typical, without a diagnosed neurodevelopmental condition. The term is used for comparison, not as a standard everyone should meet.

#### Non-Stimulant Medications

A category of ADHD medications, including atomoxetine and guanfacine, that work through mechanisms other than the immediate dopamine and norepinephrine boost of stimulants and generally take longer to reach full effect. Non-stimulants are often considered when stimulants are not tolerated, not preferred, or contraindicated.

See also: Atomoxetine, Guanfacine

#### Norepinephrine

A brain chemical involved in alertness, attention, and the body's stress response. Along with dopamine, differences in norepinephrine signaling are linked to the attention and arousal difficulties seen in ADHD.

See also: Dopamine, Arousal Regulation

#### Note-Taking Systems

Structured methods for recording information during class, meetings, or reading, designed to keep key points accessible even when sustained attention lapses during the original session. A good note-taking system for ADHD often prioritizes brevity and structure over capturing every detail.

**Example:** A man uses just three bullet points per meeting — decision, owner, deadline — instead of trying to transcribe everything that's said.

See also: Study Strategies, Externalizing Memory

#### Notification Management

The deliberate control of which alerts reach a person and when, reducing the number of interruptions competing for limited attention throughout the day. Effective notification management usually means turning off most notifications by default and allowing only a few that truly need immediate attention.

**Example:** A man turns off every app notification except calls and calendar alerts, cutting his daily phone interruptions from dozens down to a handful.

See also: Distraction Blockers, Email And Message Triage

#### Nutrition And ADHD

The relationship between diet and ADHD symptoms, an area with limited but growing evidence, where consistent meals and adequate protein may support steadier energy and focus, though no diet cures ADHD. Claims of dramatic symptom reversal through diet alone should be weighed carefully against the modest evidence available.

**Example:** A teenager who used to skip breakfast entirely finds his morning focus improves once his parents start making sure he eats something with protein before school.

See also: Omega-3 Evidence, Evaluating Treatment Claims

#### Off-Label Options

Medications prescribed for ADHD that are not specifically approved by regulators for that use but that some prescribers use based on clinical evidence or experience, particularly when standard options are not effective or tolerated. Any off-label medication decision should be discussed thoroughly with a prescriber, who weighs the available evidence for the individual case.

See also: Non-Stimulant Medications, Talking To Your Prescriber

#### Omega-3 Evidence

Research on omega-3 fatty acid supplementation for ADHD, which shows a small but measurable benefit for some symptoms in some studies, though the effect is modest compared to established treatments like medication. Omega-3s may be a reasonable supplementary addition but are not a substitute for evidence-based core treatment.

See also: Nutrition And ADHD, Supplement Claims

#### Online ADHD Culture

The body of ADHD-related content, humor, and shared experience found across social media platforms, which has significantly raised public awareness of adult and female ADHD presentations in recent years. Online content can validate real experiences, but it varies widely in accuracy and should not replace a professional evaluation.

**Example:** A teenager sees a video describing "time blindness" and immediately recognizes her own life in it for the first time, even though she'd never heard the term before.

See also: ADHD Community, ADHD Misinformation

#### Oppositional Defiant Disorder

A childhood behavioral condition marked by a persistent pattern of angry, defiant, or vindictive behavior toward authority figures, which co-occurs with ADHD more often than chance would predict. When the two occur together, treating the underlying ADHD can sometimes ease the oppositional behavior as well.

**Example:** A child with ADHD who is constantly corrected at school begins to lash out and argue with every adult who tells him what to do.

See also: Comorbidity, Emotional Dysregulation

#### Overdiagnosis Debate

The ongoing public and clinical discussion about whether ADHD is being diagnosed too readily in some populations, weighed against strong evidence of underdiagnosis in others. Research generally supports that underdiagnosis in girls, women, and adults is the larger and more consistent problem.

See also: Underdiagnosis, ADHD Misinformation

#### Parent-Child Conflict Cycles

Repeating patterns of escalating tension between a parent and a child or adult child with ADHD, often triggered by a missed task or forgotten commitment and intensified by both people's frustration. Recognizing the cycle as a pattern, rather than a personal battle each time, is often the first step toward breaking it.

**Example:** Every week, a mother reminds her son about a chore, he forgets, she gets frustrated, and both of them dread the same argument happening again.

See also: Repair After Conflict, Communication Foundations

#### Partner Communication

Ongoing, specific communication between romantic partners about how ADHD affects daily life and the relationship, aimed at addressing patterns directly rather than letting frustration build silently. Naming a specific pattern, such as forgotten chores, tends to be more productive than general complaints about effort or care.

**Example:** Instead of a vague "you never help," a husband tells his wife specifically, "when the dishes pile up for three days, I feel overwhelmed."

See also: Communication Foundations, Dividing Mental Load

#### Partner-Parent Trap

A dynamic in which a partner of someone with ADHD gradually takes on a parent-like role — reminding, managing, and correcting — which can erode both the balance of the relationship and the sense of equal partnership. Naming this trap helps couples consciously choose systems and shared responsibility over one partner quietly managing the other.

**Example:** A wife realizes she's been laying out her husband's clothes and reminding him of every appointment, more like a parent than an equal partner.

See also: Dividing Mental Load, Couples Counseling

#### Pausing Before Reacting

The practice of deliberately inserting a brief gap between feeling an urge or emotion and acting on it, creating room to choose a response rather than react automatically. This pause directly targets the weakened response inhibition involved in both impulsivity and emotional impulsivity.

**Example:** Counting to ten silently before replying to a frustrating text message.

See also: Response Inhibition, Emotional Impulsivity

#### Peer Support

Assistance and understanding offered by other people with lived experience of ADHD, whether informally or through structured programs. Peer support can reduce the isolation that often comes with ADHD, especially for those diagnosed later in life.

**Example:** Two coworkers with ADHD start quietly trading tips on which planner apps actually stick for them, without either of them ever mentioning a clinician.

See also: Support Groups, ADHD Community

#### Perfectionism

A pattern of setting excessively high standards and experiencing significant distress when they are not met. In ADHD, perfectionism can develop as a compensation strategy, since a flawless result may feel like the only way to prove competence after years of criticism.

**Example:** A man rewrites the same email eight times before sending it, terrified that one small mistake will confirm what he already fears about himself.

See also: Compensation Strategies, Years Of Negative Feedback

#### Performance Inconsistency

A pattern in which a person's output or reliability varies widely from day to day or task to task, performing excellently at times and struggling at others on seemingly similar work. This inconsistency is a hallmark of ADHD and often causes others to assume the person could always perform well if they simply chose to.

**Example:** A student aces one test after barely studying, then fails the next despite studying twice as hard, leaving her teacher confused about which version is the "real" her.

See also: Situational Variability

#### Personal Owner's Manual

A document created by a person with ADHD that lays out their own patterns, triggers, strengths, and effective strategies, intended as a practical reference for themselves and for explaining their needs to others. Building a Personal Owner's Manual turns scattered self-knowledge into one usable, sharable resource.

**Example:** A young woman writes down that she needs written instructions, not verbal ones, and that a loud fridge hum ruins her focus, then hands the page to her new roommate.

See also: Self-Advocacy, Explaining Your ADHD

#### Planner Systems

Structured tools, whether paper or digital, used to organize tasks, appointments, and deadlines in one central place. A planner system works best for ADHD when it is simple enough to maintain consistently, since an elaborate system that gets abandoned provides no benefit at all.

**Example:** A student tries four different planner apps before settling on the one simple enough that she actually opens it every day.

See also: Digital Calendars, Capture Systems

#### Planning And Prioritizing

The executive function skills involved in mapping out the steps needed to reach a goal and deciding which steps matter most. Difficulty with planning and prioritizing is a common reason tasks in ADHD feel overwhelming before they even begin.

**Example:** Someone stares at five overdue tasks and cannot tell which one is actually most urgent, so they end up doing the easiest one instead.

See also: Executive Function, Prioritization Methods

#### Pomodoro Technique

A time-management method that breaks work into fixed intervals, typically 25 minutes, followed by a short break, using a timer to structure both focus and rest. This technique can help with task initiation and sustained attention by making a work session feel finite and less overwhelming.

**Example:** A student sets a timer for 25 minutes of focused writing followed by a 5-minute break, and finds the ticking clock makes starting feel less overwhelming.

See also: Timers And Alarms, Task Initiation

#### Prefrontal Cortex

The region at the front of the brain responsible for executive functions such as planning, impulse control, and organizing behavior toward goals. Differences in the structure and activity of the prefrontal cortex are consistently linked to ADHD.

See also: Executive Function, Brain Networks

#### Pregnancy And ADHD

The period during pregnancy when ADHD symptom management often changes, partly due to hormonal shifts and partly because many people pause or adjust medication in consultation with their prescriber. Decisions about medication during pregnancy should always be made with a prescriber, weighing individual risks and benefits.

**Example:** A pregnant woman and her prescriber weigh together whether to continue her stimulant medication, balancing her functioning against the pregnancy.

See also: Hormones And ADHD, Talking To Your Prescriber

#### Preparing For An Evaluation

The steps a person can take before an ADHD evaluation, such as gathering old report cards, making notes on daily struggles, or asking a family member for their observations. Good preparation gives the clinician more accurate material to work with and can shorten the overall process.

See also: Collateral Information, Diagnostic Evaluation

#### Primary Care Providers

Doctors or nurse practitioners who deliver general medical care and, in many cases, can screen for, diagnose, or manage ongoing treatment of ADHD, sometimes referring complex cases to a specialist. For many adults, a primary care provider is the first and most accessible point of contact for raising concerns about ADHD.

See also: Who Can Diagnose ADHD, Finding A Clinician

#### Prioritization Methods

Structured approaches for deciding which tasks matter most and should be done first, addressing a common ADHD difficulty in weighing competing demands. Simple, visual prioritization methods tend to work better for ADHD than complex systems that themselves require significant planning effort to use.

**Example:** A man circles just one task each morning as the single thing that must get done, so a long list doesn't leave him frozen about where to start.

See also: Planning And Prioritizing, Deadline Scaffolding

#### Procrastination

Delaying the start or completion of a task despite knowing the delay will cause problems later. In ADHD, procrastination is usually driven by task-initiation and motivation difficulties rather than poor time management alone.

**Example:** A woman knows a report is due Friday and spends Wednesday reorganizing her desk instead of opening the document.

See also: Task Initiation, Momentum And Activation

#### Prospective Memory

The ability to remember to carry out a planned action at the right future moment, such as taking medication or returning a call. Prospective memory is one of the memory systems most affected by ADHD, independent of general intelligence or long-term memory ability.

**Example:** Setting a full water glass by the door to remember to bring it to a friend, then walking out the door without it anyway.

See also: Working Memory, Externalizing Memory

#### Psychiatrists

Medical doctors who specialize in diagnosing and treating mental health conditions, including ADHD, and who can prescribe medication.

#### Psychoeducation

The process of teaching a person and their family accurate, practical information about ADHD — what it is, how it affects daily life, and what treatments exist. Psychoeducation is often the first step in treatment, since understanding the condition shapes how well other strategies are accepted and used.

See also: Family Psychoeducation, Evidence-Based Information

#### Psychologists

Licensed professionals trained in assessing and treating mental health conditions through evaluation and therapy, who in most regions cannot prescribe medication. Psychologists often lead the testing portion of a comprehensive ADHD evaluation.

#### Reading With ADHD

The particular challenges ADHD can create around sustained reading, such as rereading the same paragraph repeatedly because attention drifted mid-sentence. Strategies like reading aloud, using a finger or pointer to track lines, or breaking reading into short timed sessions can noticeably improve comprehension.

**Example:** A woman finds she finally finishes a chapter once she starts running her finger under each line instead of letting her eyes drift on their own.

See also: Mind Wandering, Study Strategies

#### Reframing Your Past

The process of looking back on earlier struggles, failures, or labels — like "lazy" or "not living up to potential" — through the lens of a new ADHD diagnosis. Reframing does not erase past pain, but it can replace self-blame with a more accurate explanation.

**Example:** A woman looks back at her "lazy" college transcript and, for the first time, sees a brain working against unmanaged ADHD instead of a person who didn't care.

See also: Grief After Late Diagnosis, Identity After Diagnosis

#### Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria

A term used within the ADHD community and by some clinicians to describe intense emotional pain in response to perceived criticism, rejection, or failure, out of proportion to the triggering event. Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria is not a formal diagnosis in the DSM-5, but it names an experience many people with ADHD find validating and useful to have a word for.

**Example:** Replaying a friend's slightly short text message for hours, certain it means the friendship is over.

See also: Emotional Dysregulation, Shame And ADHD

#### Reminder Apps

Software tools that send timed alerts for tasks or appointments, functioning as an external prompt for a working memory that may not reliably generate its own. Reminder apps work best when notifications are specific and timed to when action is actually needed, not just when a task was originally added.

**Example:** A man sets a recurring alert for "take out the trash" every Tuesday night, since relying on memory alone meant missing pickup half the time.

See also: Digital Calendars, Notification Management

#### Reminders Without Nagging

Approaches to prompting a family member with ADHD about a task or commitment in a way that avoids the tone of repeated criticism, which tends to damage trust without actually improving follow-through. Reminders framed as a neutral, agreed-upon system tend to land better than reminders delivered as frustration in the moment.

**Example:** A couple agrees on one shared reminder app instead of one partner repeatedly asking the other, "did you do it yet?"

See also: Communication Foundations, Dividing Household Labor

#### Remote Work And ADHD

The particular advantages and challenges ADHD can bring to working from home, including fewer environmental distractions and more schedule flexibility, but also reduced external structure and increased isolation. Remote work can be especially good or especially hard for ADHD, depending heavily on how well a person builds their own structure to replace what an office provided.

**Example:** A woman finds she's far more productive without office chatter, but has to build in her own structure since no one's walking by to check on her progress.

See also: Environment Design, Routines And Habits

#### Repair After Conflict

The deliberate process of reconnecting and addressing what went wrong after a conflict, rather than letting tension linger unaddressed. Repair matters especially in ADHD-affected families, where the same type of conflict can recur often enough to erode trust if it is never actively resolved.

**Example:** After a heated argument over a missed appointment, a father sits down with his daughter later that evening to talk through what happened, instead of letting it go unspoken.

See also: Parent-Child Conflict Cycles, Family Meetings

#### Requesting Accommodations

The formal process of asking a school, college, or workplace for adjustments needed because of ADHD, typically requiring documentation such as an evaluation report. Knowing the specific process and required paperwork in advance makes requesting accommodations far less daunting.

**Example:** A father gathers his son's evaluation report and requests a 504 Plan meeting with the school, using the report as the required documentation.

See also: Evaluation Report, School Accommodations

#### Resilience

The capacity to recover from setbacks and keep functioning despite ongoing difficulty, often built up over years by people with ADHD navigating a world not designed around their needs. Recognizing this resilience as a real, earned strength counters the narrative that ADHD only produces deficits.

**Example:** A woman has been fired twice and started over twice, and still walks into her third job with the same determination to make it work.

See also: ADHD Strengths, Self-Compassion

#### Respite And Boundaries

Planned breaks from caregiving responsibilities, combined with clear limits on what a caregiver will and will not take on, both aimed at preventing caregiver burnout. Setting a boundary is not a withdrawal of care — it is what makes ongoing care possible.

**Example:** A mother tells her extended family she won't be answering calls about her son's ADHD-related issues after 8 p.m., protecting a few hours to herself each night.

See also: Caregiver Self-Care, Caregiver Burnout

#### Response Inhibition

The ability to stop an automatic or impulsive reaction before acting on it. Difficulty with response inhibition underlies much of the impulsivity seen in ADHD.

**Example:** Interrupting a conversation because the thought could not be held back until it was your turn to speak.

See also: Impulsivity, Interrupting

#### Restlessness In Adults

The adult expression of hyperactivity, which more often appears as an internal sense of unease, a need to stay busy, or a preference for constant activity rather than visible physical movement like running or climbing. Recognizing this shift matters because clinicians once assumed hyperactivity always fades with age.

**Example:** A man can't sit through a two-hour movie without getting up twice, even though nothing about the film itself bothers him.

See also: Internal Restlessness, ADHD In Adulthood

#### Reward System

The brain circuitry that processes pleasure, motivation, and reinforcement, guiding which behaviors a person repeats. In ADHD, this system responds less strongly to delayed or small rewards, which shapes both motivation and the drive toward immediately interesting activities.

See also: Dopamine, Interest-Based Nervous System

#### Reward Systems

Structured plans that pair completing a task with a meaningful, immediate reward, taking advantage of how the ADHD brain responds more strongly to near-term than distant incentives. Reward systems work best when the reward is small, prompt, and tied directly to the specific task being encouraged.

**Example:** A woman lets herself watch one episode of a show only after she finishes folding the laundry, giving her brain an immediate reason to follow through.

See also: Reward System, Habit Stacking

#### Routines And Habits

Regularly repeated sequences of behavior performed with little conscious planning, which reduce the number of decisions and task initiations a person needs to make each day. Because ADHD makes each individual decision more effortful, well-built routines can lighten the daily cognitive load considerably.

**Example:** A man no longer has to decide whether to take his medication each morning — it's simply part of the same three steps he does right after brushing his teeth.

See also: Habit Stacking, Morning Routines

#### School Accommodations

Formal adjustments made to a student's educational environment or expectations, such as extended test time or preferential seating, intended to reduce the impact of ADHD on academic performance. In the United States, school accommodations are typically formalized through an IEP or a 504 Plan.

**Example:** A student is allowed to take tests in a quiet side room instead of the noisy main classroom, once her accommodations are formally approved.

See also: IEP, 504 Plan

#### Screening For Comorbidities

The practice of checking for other conditions, such as anxiety, depression, or a learning disability, at the same time as evaluating for ADHD. Screening for comorbidities matters because untreated co-occurring conditions can undermine the effectiveness of ADHD treatment alone.

See also: Comorbidity, Differential Diagnosis

#### Second Opinions

Seeking evaluation or advice from an additional clinician when a diagnosis, or the absence of one, does not match a person's lived experience. Getting a second opinion is a legitimate and common step, especially given how often ADHD is missed on a first evaluation.

See also: Misdiagnosis, Finding A Clinician

#### Self-Advocacy

The practice of speaking up for one's own needs, whether requesting an accommodation, explaining a limitation, or correcting a misconception about ADHD. Self-advocacy is a learnable skill that becomes easier with practice and a clear understanding of one's own needs.

**Example:** A man tells his boss plainly, "I focus better with written instructions than verbal ones," instead of quietly struggling and staying silent about it.

See also: Explaining Your ADHD, Requesting Accommodations

#### Self-Compassion

The practice of treating oneself with the same understanding and patience one would offer a friend, particularly after a mistake or setback. Self-compassion is a practical counter to the shame that often builds up around ADHD-related struggles, replacing self-criticism with a more accurate and kinder view.

**Example:** After missing another appointment, a man tells himself "that was a hard morning, not a character flaw" instead of spiraling into self-criticism.

See also: Shame And ADHD, Cognitive Reframing

#### Self-Esteem And ADHD

The impact of living with ADHD, especially before diagnosis, on a person's overall sense of self-worth and competence. Because so much of daily life depends on executive function, chronic struggles with organization and follow-through can quietly erode self-esteem over many years.

**Example:** A man assumes he's simply "not smart enough" for a promotion, when the real issue has always been unmanaged executive function, not ability.

See also: Shame And ADHD, Imposter Feelings

#### Self-Monitoring

The ongoing ability to observe one's own behavior, performance, and impact on others while a task is happening. Weaker self-monitoring in ADHD can mean not noticing a mistake, a tone of voice, or the passage of time until it becomes a problem.

**Example:** A student doesn't notice he's been tapping his pen loudly through an entire meeting until a classmate finally asks him to stop.

See also: Executive Function, Time Blindness

#### Self-Report Questionnaires

Standardized forms in which a person answers questions about their own symptoms and functioning, commonly used in adult ADHD evaluations where childhood records or teacher reports may not be available. Self-report questionnaires are useful but are usually paired with other sources of information for accuracy.

See also: ADHD Rating Scales, Collateral Information

#### Sensory Sensitivity

A heightened or uneven response to sensory input such as sound, light, texture, or touch, which many people with ADHD report even though it is not part of the formal diagnostic criteria. Sensory sensitivity can intensify distractibility and contribute to overwhelm in busy environments.

**Example:** A child melts down at a birthday party not because of the games, but because the room is loud, bright, and impossible to filter out.

See also: Distractibility, Cognitive Load

#### Shame And ADHD

The persistent feeling of being fundamentally flawed or inadequate that can build up after years of struggling with tasks others seem to manage easily. Shame in ADHD often has less to do with any single failure and more to do with a lifetime pattern of negative feedback misread as evidence of poor character.

**Example:** A woman apologizes reflexively for being "so annoying" every time she forgets something small, long after the people around her have stopped minding.

See also: Years Of Negative Feedback, Self-Esteem And ADHD

#### Shared Family Calendars

A calendar system, digital or physical, used by all family members to track shared appointments, deadlines, and responsibilities in one visible place. A shared calendar reduces the burden on any single family member's memory and cuts down on missed commitments that affect the whole household.

**Example:** A family puts every practice, appointment, and deadline on one shared calendar app, so no single parent has to remember it all alone.

See also: Digital Calendars, Family Meetings

#### Side Effects

Unintended physical or emotional effects that can occur alongside a medication's intended benefits, such as appetite changes, sleep difficulty, or irritability with ADHD medications. Most side effects are manageable through dose or timing adjustments made together with a prescriber.

**Example:** A woman notices she isn't hungry at lunch anymore after starting medication, so she sets a reminder to eat even when she doesn't feel like it.

See also: Appetite And Sleep Effects, Cardiovascular Considerations

#### Situational Variability

The way ADHD symptoms can appear stronger in some settings and barely noticeable in others, depending on structure, interest, and stakes. This variability sometimes leads others to doubt the diagnosis, mistaking situational ease for proof the person could always perform this way if they tried.

**Example:** A student who cannot focus in a lecture hall but concentrates fully during a one-on-one video game session.

See also: Interest-Based Nervous System, Hyperfocus

#### Sleep And The ADHD Brain

The well-documented connection between ADHD and sleep difficulties, including trouble falling asleep, delayed body clocks, and poorer sleep quality, which can worsen attention and mood the next day. Sleep problems in ADHD often go both ways — poor sleep worsens symptoms, and ADHD makes good sleep harder to achieve.

**Example:** A woman lies in bed exhausted at midnight but her mind won't quiet down, so she scrolls her phone until 2 a.m., then struggles to wake for work.

See also: Delayed Sleep Phase Syndrome, Sleep Hygiene

#### Sleep Disorders

Conditions that disrupt the quality, timing, or amount of sleep, which occur at higher rates in people with ADHD and can worsen attention and mood the next day. Treating a co-occurring sleep disorder can sometimes improve ADHD symptoms as much as adjusting ADHD treatment itself.

**Example:** A woman is treated for insomnia for years before anyone connects it to her untreated ADHD, which was keeping her mind too active to wind down.

See also: Sleep And The ADHD Brain, Delayed Sleep Phase Syndrome

#### Sleep Hygiene

A set of habits and environmental adjustments, such as a consistent bedtime and reduced screen use before bed, that support better sleep quality. Good sleep hygiene is especially valuable for people with ADHD, since poor sleep tends to worsen attention and emotional regulation the next day.

**Example:** A woman starts putting her phone in another room an hour before bed, and finds she falls asleep faster within a week.

See also: Sleep And The ADHD Brain, Delayed Sleep Phase Syndrome

#### Spaced Repetition

A study technique that schedules review of material at gradually increasing intervals over time, rather than in one concentrated session. Spaced repetition compensates for working-memory limits by rebuilding a memory just as it starts to fade, rather than relying on a single intense study effort.

**Example:** A student reviews new vocabulary the same day, then two days later, then a week later, instead of cramming it all the night before the test.

See also: Study Strategies, Active Recall

#### Starting Rituals

A short, consistent sequence of actions performed right before beginning a task, used to signal to the brain that it's time to shift into work mode. Starting rituals can lower the activation energy needed for task initiation by making the moment of starting smaller and more familiar.

**Example:** Clearing a desk and setting a timer for ten minutes before beginning any writing task.

See also: Task Initiation, Momentum And Activation

#### Stimulant Medications

A class of medications, including methylphenidate and amphetamine compounds, that increase the availability of dopamine and norepinephrine in the brain and are considered the first-line, most extensively studied treatment for ADHD. Despite their name, stimulants do not cause euphoria at prescribed doses in people with ADHD — they support focus and impulse control.

See also: Methylphenidate, Amphetamine Medications

#### Stimulant Misconceptions

Common but inaccurate beliefs about stimulant medications, such as the idea that they are equivalent to recreational drugs or inevitably lead to addiction. Research consistently shows that properly prescribed and monitored stimulant treatment is safe and does not increase, and may reduce, the risk of later substance misuse.

**Example:** A relative warns a newly diagnosed adult that stimulants will turn her into "a different person" or get her addicted, based on nothing but rumor.

See also: Substance Use Risk, Controlled Substance Rules

#### Study Strategies

Approaches to learning and reviewing material that account for ADHD-related challenges with sustained attention and working memory, such as active recall and spaced repetition rather than passive rereading. Study strategies built around how the ADHD brain actually retains information tend to outperform generic study advice.

**Example:** Instead of rereading her notes for hours, a student switches to quizzing herself with flashcards and finds she actually remembers the material.

See also: Active Recall, Spaced Repetition

#### Substance Use Risk

The elevated likelihood, documented in research, of developing problematic alcohol or drug use among people with untreated ADHD, often connected to self-medicating symptoms like restlessness or low mood. Appropriately treating ADHD, including with medication, has been shown to lower this elevated risk rather than increase it.

**Example:** A young man starts drinking most nights in college, unknowingly using alcohol to quiet a restless, unmedicated ADHD brain.

See also: Comorbidity, Stimulant Misconceptions

#### Supplement Claims

Marketing statements made about vitamins, minerals, or other supplements said to treat or cure ADHD, most of which are not held to the same evidence standards as approved medications. Supplement claims should be evaluated with particular caution, since supplements are not required to prove effectiveness before being sold.

See also: Evaluating Treatment Claims, Omega-3 Evidence

#### Support For Caregivers

Resources, including support groups, counseling, and educational materials, designed specifically to help the family members of someone with ADHD rather than the person with ADHD directly. Recognizing caregivers as needing their own support is part of treating ADHD as a condition that affects the whole family system.

**Example:** A father joins an online group specifically for parents of kids with ADHD, finding relief in talking to people who understand the exhaustion without judgment.

See also: Caregiver Strain, Family Psychoeducation

#### Support Groups

Organized gatherings, in person or online, where people with ADHD or their family members share experiences and strategies with others facing similar challenges. Support groups provide connection and practical tips but are not a substitute for professional treatment.

**Example:** A newly diagnosed woman joins a monthly meetup and, for the first time, hears someone else describe the exact same "why can't I just start" feeling she thought was hers alone.

See also: Peer Support, ADHD Community

#### Supporting Without Parenting

The practice, particularly relevant for family members of an adult with ADHD, of offering help and structure without taking over responsibilities the adult is capable of managing themselves. This distinction protects an adult's autonomy while still allowing meaningful support from people who care about them.

**Example:** A mother resists the urge to call her adult son every morning to remind him about a bill, instead helping him set up an automatic reminder he manages himself.

See also: Adult Child Autonomy, Reminders Without Nagging

#### Symptom Criteria

The specific behaviors listed in the DSM-5 that a clinician checks for when considering an ADHD diagnosis, divided into inattentive and hyperactive-impulsive symptom lists. A set minimum number of symptoms from these lists must be present, along with impairment, to meet diagnostic criteria.

See also: DSM-5 Definition Of ADHD, Symptom Thresholds

#### Symptom Thresholds

The minimum number of symptoms from the DSM-5 lists required for a diagnosis — five for adults, six for children, in either the inattentive or hyperactive-impulsive category. These thresholds are the same for everyone, but symptoms can look different depending on age, sex, and coping strategies.

See also: Symptom Criteria, Age Of Onset Criterion

#### System Maintenance

The ongoing effort required to keep an organizational system, like a planner or a decluttered space, working over time rather than gradually falling apart. Building in a regular, simple maintenance check is often what separates a system that lasts from one that is abandoned after a few weeks.

See also: Planner Systems, Decluttering For ADHD

#### Talking To Your Prescriber

The ongoing conversation between a patient and their prescriber about symptoms, side effects, and treatment goals, which shapes decisions about dose, medication type, and other treatment components. Being specific and honest in these conversations, including about missed doses or unwanted effects, leads to better-tailored treatment.

**Example:** A woman finally admits to her doctor that she's been skipping her afternoon dose because it makes her jittery, instead of just enduring it silently.

See also: Tracking Medication Response, Deciding About Medication

#### Task Breakdown

The practice of dividing a large or vague task into smaller, clearly defined steps that each feel achievable to begin. Task breakdown directly addresses the task-initiation difficulty common in ADHD, since a small first step is far easier to start than an open-ended project.

**Example:** "Write the report" becomes "open a blank document," "write one sentence," and "write the next paragraph" — three steps small enough to actually start.

See also: Task Initiation, Defining Next Actions

#### Task Initiation

The ability to start a task without excessive delay, once it is understood and intended. Task initiation is often the single hardest step in ADHD, even for tasks the person genuinely wants to complete.

**Example:** A woman knows she needs to start her taxes, wants to start her taxes, and still finds herself scrolling her phone an hour later, no closer to starting.

See also: Procrastination, Starting Rituals

#### Task Switching Difficulty

Trouble disengaging from one task and fully engaging with another, which can create a lag or loss of momentum every time attention needs to move. This can make multitasking and interruptions especially costly for someone with ADHD.

**Example:** Pulled away from a spreadsheet to answer a coworker's quick question, a man loses ten minutes trying to remember what he was doing before the interruption.

See also: Cognitive Flexibility, Momentum And Activation

#### Telehealth Evaluations

ADHD assessments and follow-up appointments conducted remotely by video rather than in person, which expanded significantly and became more accepted after 2020. Telehealth has improved access to evaluation for people in areas with few nearby specialists, though some jurisdictions place limits on prescribing controlled substances this way.

See also: Finding A Clinician, Controlled Substance Rules

#### Telling A New Partner

The decision of when and how to disclose an ADHD diagnosis to a new romantic partner, which is a personal choice with no single correct timing. Framing the conversation around practical impact, such as how attention or memory show up day to day, tends to be more useful to a partner than the label alone.

**Example:** A woman waits until the third date to explain her ADHD, choosing a calm moment rather than blurting it out during an early awkward silence.

See also: ADHD And Dating, Disclosure To Friends

#### Test-Taking Strategies

Approaches to exam performance that address ADHD-related challenges, such as reading all questions before starting, using extended time when available, and working through easier questions first to build momentum. These strategies aim to reduce the impact of time pressure and distractibility on demonstrating actual knowledge.

**Example:** A student reads through the entire exam once before answering anything, so a hard question early on doesn't eat up all her remaining time.

See also: School Accommodations, Momentum And Activation

#### Tic Disorders

Conditions marked by sudden, repetitive, non-rhythmic movements or vocalizations, which occur more frequently in people with ADHD than in the general population. Because certain stimulant medications can occasionally worsen tics, prescribers weigh this history when choosing a treatment plan.

**Example:** A boy on a stimulant develops a new eye-blinking tic, prompting his parents and prescriber to discuss whether to adjust his medication.

See also: Comorbidity, Medication And Comorbidities

#### Time Blindness

Difficulty sensing how much time has passed or how much remains, making it hard to estimate, track, or plan around time without external cues. Time blindness is a common and often misunderstood ADHD trait, frequently mistaken for not caring about being on time.

**Example:** Believing only twenty minutes have passed when in fact two hours have gone by.

See also: Time Estimation, Hyperfocus

#### Time Estimation

The skill of accurately predicting how long a task will take to complete, which is frequently distorted in ADHD due to time blindness. Poor time estimation often leads to a cycle of underestimating how long tasks take and running chronically late as a result.

**Example:** A woman guesses a task will take twenty minutes and is surprised, again, when it takes two hours, so she starts doubling her estimates by habit.

See also: Time Blindness, Deadline Scaffolding

#### Timers And Alarms

External tools used to mark the passage of time or signal when to start, stop, or switch a task, compensating for the internal difficulty many people with ADHD have sensing time. Timers work best when set for concrete, specific actions rather than vague reminders to "check in."

**Example:** A man sets a timer labeled "leave for the dentist" fifteen minutes before he actually needs to walk out the door, since a vague sense of time isn't enough.

See also: Time Blindness, Pomodoro Technique

#### Titration

The gradual process of adjusting a medication's dose, usually starting low and increasing over time, to find the amount that provides the best symptom control with the fewest side effects. Titration is a normal part of starting ADHD medication and typically requires several follow-up appointments.

**Example:** A prescriber starts a patient on a low dose of medication and checks in every two weeks, nudging the amount up slightly until focus improves without troubling side effects.

See also: Finding The Right Dose, Side Effects

#### To-Do Lists That Work

Task lists structured in a way that accounts for ADHD-related challenges, such as keeping the list short, specific, and visible, rather than long and abstract. A to-do list that works is usually reviewed at set times during the day, not left to be checked only when remembered.

**Example:** A man switches from one long, ever-growing list to a short list of just three items each morning, and finally starts finishing what's on it.

See also: Task Breakdown, Prioritization Methods

#### Tracking Medication Response

The practice of monitoring how well an ADHD medication is working and what side effects appear, often using notes, apps, or rating scales shared with a prescriber. Tracking response over time gives a prescriber the information needed to fine-tune dose and timing.

**Example:** A man keeps a simple note on his phone each week rating his focus and mood, so his prescriber has real data instead of a vague "it's fine" at each visit.

See also: Finding The Right Dose, Measuring Treatment Progress

#### Transition To College

The shift from a structured, closely supervised high school environment to the greater independence and looser oversight of college life. This transition is a common point at which previously compensated ADHD symptoms become disruptive enough to prompt an evaluation.

**Example:** A freshman who always had a parent checking his homework folder now has no one checking anything, and three assignments slip through the cracks in his first month.

See also: ADHD In College Years, College Disability Services

#### Transition To Work

The shift from the academic structure of school into the different demands of a workplace, including new expectations around punctuality, self-direction, and long-term planning. Many adults first notice how much ADHD affects them during this transition, once external structure decreases.

**Example:** A new graduate who thrived with school deadlines and semesters finds the open-ended, self-directed pace of her first job far harder to structure on her own.

See also: ADHD In Adulthood, Career Fit

#### Trauma And ADHD

The relationship between traumatic experiences and ADHD, which can occur together, resemble each other in some symptoms like difficulty concentrating, and even influence each other's severity. Careful evaluation is needed because trauma-related symptoms and ADHD symptoms can be mistaken for one another.

**Example:** A therapist works carefully to figure out whether a child's inability to concentrate in class comes from ADHD, from an unstable home situation, or from both at once.

See also: Chronic Stress Effects, Differential Diagnosis

#### Treatment Evidence Hierarchy

A framework for ranking the strength of evidence behind a treatment, from large randomized controlled trials at the top down to individual testimonials at the bottom. Using this hierarchy helps separate well-supported ADHD treatments from those resting mainly on anecdote.

See also: Evidence-Based Information, Evaluating Treatment Claims

#### Treatment Order Decisions

Clinical judgments about which condition to treat first when a person has ADHD along with a co-occurring condition, such as treating severe depression before starting ADHD medication. These decisions are made case by case, since the right order depends on which condition is causing the most immediate impairment.

See also: Integrated Treatment Planning, Comorbidity

#### Underdiagnosis

The pattern of ADHD going unrecognized in groups where symptoms present differently than the stereotype clinicians were trained to spot, especially girls, women, and adults with strong compensation strategies. Underdiagnosis means many people live for years without access to support that could have helped them.

See also: Gender Diagnosis Gap, Masking

#### Visual Reminders

Reminders placed where they can be seen repeatedly throughout the day, such as sticky notes or whiteboards, taking advantage of visible cues rather than relying on memory alone to prompt action. Visual reminders work especially well for tasks that are easy to forget simply because they are out of sight.

**Example:** A sticky note on the bathroom mirror reminds a teenager to pack her gym clothes, since a note buried in her phone would never get seen in time.

See also: Environment Design, Reminder Apps

#### What A Diagnosis Means

The practical and personal significance of receiving an ADHD diagnosis: access to treatment and accommodations, a framework for understanding past struggles, and the start of building a more effective set of supports. A diagnosis describes a pattern of brain-based difference — it does not define a person's worth or potential.

See also: Reframing Your Past, Identity After Diagnosis

#### When To Seek Help

The point at which ADHD-related struggles are significant or persistent enough to warrant a professional evaluation, generally when symptoms are causing real difficulty at school, work, or in relationships. There is no need to wait for a crisis — ongoing impairment is reason enough to seek an evaluation.

**Example:** A man finally books an evaluation after missing a third work deadline in a month, deciding the pattern is now too consistent to ignore.

See also: Diagnostic Evaluation, Finding A Clinician

#### Who Can Diagnose ADHD

The range of licensed professionals qualified to evaluate and diagnose ADHD, including psychiatrists, psychologists, and some primary care providers, depending on training and local regulations. Knowing this range helps people find an appropriate clinician instead of assuming only one type of specialist can help.

See also: Psychiatrists, Psychologists

#### Willpower Myth

The false belief that ADHD symptoms can be overcome simply by trying harder or wanting success badly enough. This myth ignores that ADHD involves measurable differences in brain chemistry and structure that willpower alone does not change.

**Example:** A father tells his son to "just focus harder" on his homework, not realizing the boy has already tried everything he knows how to try.

See also: Laziness Myth, ADHD As Regulation Difference

#### Working Memory

The ability to hold information in mind and use it briefly while completing a task, such as remembering a set of instructions long enough to follow them. Weak working memory is one of the most consistent difficulties found in ADHD.

**Example:** Walking into a room to get something and forgetting what it was by the time you arrive.

See also: Executive Function, Prospective Memory

#### Working With A Manager

The relationship between an employee with ADHD and their supervisor, which can be improved by clear, specific communication about how instructions, deadlines, and feedback are best delivered and received. A good working relationship with a manager often depends less on disclosure and more on establishing practical communication habits.

**Example:** An employee asks his manager to send follow-up instructions by email after every conversation, since he tends to lose details from spoken requests alone.

See also: Workplace Disclosure, Handling Feedback At Work

#### Workplace Accommodations

Adjustments an employer provides to help an employee with ADHD perform their job effectively, such as written instructions, noise-reducing headphones, or flexible deadlines. In the U.S., workplace accommodations are generally requested and protected under the ADA.

**Example:** An employer agrees to give a woman written follow-ups after every verbal meeting, since she processes instructions on paper better than out loud.

See also: ADA Basics, Workplace Disclosure

#### Workplace Disclosure

The personal decision of whether, when, and how much to tell an employer about an ADHD diagnosis, which is generally required only if formally requesting accommodations under the ADA. Disclosure is optional in most everyday situations and depends on what an individual judges will help versus create risk in their specific workplace.

**Example:** A man decides to tell only his direct manager about his ADHD, specifically to formally request noise-canceling headphones at his desk.

See also: ADA Basics, Self-Advocacy

#### Writing With ADHD

The particular challenges ADHD can create around producing written work, including difficulty organizing ideas, starting a first draft, and staying on topic. Breaking writing into small, defined stages, such as outlining, drafting, and editing as separate steps, can make the process far more manageable.

**Example:** A student who freezes staring at a blank page finds it easier to first record a rough voice memo of her ideas, then turn that into an outline.

See also: Task Breakdown, Task Initiation

#### Years Of Negative Feedback

The cumulative effect of receiving repeated criticism, correction, or discipline over childhood and adulthood because of ADHD symptoms that were not recognized or understood at the time. This accumulated feedback is a major, well-documented contributor to the anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem that often accompany ADHD.

**Example:** By the time she's thirty, a woman has heard some version of "if you'd just try harder" so many times from teachers and bosses that she's started to believe it herself.

See also: Shame And ADHD, Depression
