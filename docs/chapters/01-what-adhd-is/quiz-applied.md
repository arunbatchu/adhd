# Applied Quiz: What ADHD Is And Is Not

A second set of questions on this chapter, weighted toward applying the ideas
rather than recalling them. Each one gives you a situation and asks what the
chapter's framework says about it.

---

#### 1. Your brother plays guitar for five hours straight but cannot start a twenty-minute insurance form. A relative says this proves he could focus "if he actually wanted to." Using this chapter's framework, what is the accurate reading?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The relative is right — sustained guitar practice proves the attention capacity is there and available on demand
2. His attention capacity is fine; what is inconsistent is the steering, which pulls hard toward interest and novelty and barely tugs toward flat, no-deadline tasks
3. He has two different conditions — one affecting hobbies, one affecting paperwork
4. The guitar session means he does not meet the criteria for ADHD
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. This is the regulation lens applied to exactly the situation it was built for — the same pattern as Maya's six-hour coding session and the unrenewed car registration. People with ADHD do not have a shortage of attention; the steering is what's uneven. Option A is the "he could if he wanted to" conclusion the chapter identifies as the natural but wrong one. Option C invents complexity the single regulation frame already explains. Option D gets it backwards — this inconsistency is characteristic of ADHD, not evidence against it.

#### 2. A father wants to help his son with an unpaid bill that has sat on the counter for three weeks. Which response applies the regulation lens rather than the deficit lens?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Ask him each evening whether he has paid it yet
2. Explain how disappointing the lapse is, so the stakes register
3. Change the task's pull — set up autopay, or move the bill to the coffee maker, or attach it to a standing Sunday ritual
4. Wait for the late fee, on the theory that consequences teach
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter's Sam example makes exactly this contrast: a deficit lens says the person lacks attention, so the fix is to demand more of it (reminders, lectures, hurt feelings). A regulation lens says the steering doesn't pull toward flat no-deadline paper, so the fix is to change the task's pull by adding urgency, visibility, or interest. Options A and B are the demand-more-attention responses, and the chapter shows they turn into a fight about whether he cares. Option D leaves the steering problem untouched and adds a cost.

    **Concept Tested:** ADHD As Regulation Difference

    **See:** [Chapter 1](index.md)

#### 3. An 8-year-old with ADHD is told to wait ten minutes for dessert and is in the kitchen within ninety seconds. Which analysis matches the chapter's delayed-maturation finding?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. He is testing the rule deliberately and needs a firmer consequence
2. The wait ran on self-control machinery that is developmentally two to three years younger than his age, so the expectation exceeded the hardware
3. His brain will not develop this capacity, so waiting tasks should be avoided permanently
4. Ninety seconds is within the normal range for any 8-year-old
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Imaging studies following thousands of children found the cortex reaches its milestones on the same trajectory but roughly two to three years later, which is why an 8-year-old with ADHD can resemble a typical 5- or 6-year-old in self-control terms. Option A reads defiance into a developmental gap. Option C overshoots — the chapter says some of the gap narrows with maturation, though some differences persist. Option D ignores the comparison the chapter draws with the child who waits, complaining.

    **Concept Tested:** ADHD Brain Maturation Lag

    **See:** [Chapter 1](index.md)

#### 4. A 41-year-old is diagnosed with ADHD after a lifetime of no diagnosis. A colleague says adult-onset ADHD is a fad. What does the chapter's definition establish?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The colleague is correct, since ADHD is a childhood condition people outgrow
2. Adult-onset ADHD is a recognized separate category with its own criteria
3. A diagnosis after 30 is provisional until childhood records are located
4. Diagnostic rules require several signs present before age 12, so what is new at 41 is the explanation, not the ADHD
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The chapter is explicit: ADHD doesn't appear in adulthood out of nowhere, and a late diagnosis is a re-reading of a whole life story — the lost homework, the report cards pleading for more effort, the chaos always narrowly managed. Option A contradicts ADHD being a lifespan condition. Option B invents a category the DSM-5 does not have. Option C overstates the requirement; childhood evidence is gathered in various ways, and the diagnosis is not held provisional pending records.

    **Concept Tested:** DSM-5 Definition Of ADHD

    **See:** [Chapter 1](index.md)

#### 5. A student meets seven inattention symptoms, has had them for a year, and struggles badly — but only in one difficult class, with no problems anywhere else. What does the DSM-5's counting logic say?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The multiple-settings requirement is not met, so this picture does not satisfy the criteria as stated
2. Seven symptoms exceeds the threshold, so the other requirements are waived
3. One setting is sufficient as long as the impairment there is severe
4. The duration requirement fails, since one year is too short
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. All five counting rules must be met together, and the multiple-settings rule exists precisely to exclude this case — symptoms in two or more settings, "not just one difficult classroom." Options B and C treat the symptom count as able to override the other requirements, which is exactly what the counting rules prevent. Option D is wrong on the facts: the duration requirement is six months, and one year exceeds it.

    **Concept Tested:** DSM-5 Definition Of ADHD

    **See:** [Chapter 1](index.md)

#### 7. A parent reads that ADHD is "just a different kind of brain, not a disorder," and concludes their daughter needs no support at school. How does the chapter's strengths-honest position assess this?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Correct — neurodiversity means the impairments are a matter of perspective
2. Incomplete — ADHD is a difference that can be genuinely disabling, and treating it as merely a difference insults how hard the struggle can be
3. Correct, provided the school environment is well designed
4. Incorrect, because the neurodiversity framing has no place in an evidence-based book
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter takes both halves of the sentence seriously: ADHD is a difference that carries genuine strengths in some environments and genuine impairments in others, and it says plainly that pretending the impairments are merely differences insults how hard the struggle can be. Options A and C keep only the flattering half. Option D discards the framing entirely, which the chapter also declines to do — it presents neurodiversity as part of natural human variation.

    **Concept Tested:** Neurodiversity

#### 8. Filling out a form at 8 pm, a person sits down, gets stuck on question 12, ignores a phone buzz, then goes to stir dinner and finds the form untouched three hours later. Which attention skill failed at the final step?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Selecting — choosing the form over the phone
2. Resisting capture — keeping the buzz as background noise
3. Shifting — deliberately moving the beam and then actually settling back on the original target
4. Dividing — juggling two targets at once
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter's worked example maps each step to a named skill, and the stove moment is shifting: going to the stove and then *actually coming back to question 12*, rather than discovering the form three hours later. Options A and B name skills the person succeeded at earlier in the same evening. Option D describes juggling two targets simultaneously, like driving while following spoken directions, which is not what happened here.

    **Concept Tested:** Attention

    **See:** [Chapter 1](index.md)

#### 9. A teacher hides her suspected ADHD for three more years rather than seeking evaluation. Which sequence best analyzes the cost, per the chapter's stigma cycle?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Hiding delays evaluation, so the struggle continues unexplained, so myths like laziness supply the explanation, and those explanations get internalized as self-stigma
2. Hiding reduces the symptoms temporarily, so evaluation becomes unnecessary
3. Hiding affects only how others see her, leaving her own self-assessment untouched
4. Hiding accelerates diagnosis once the symptoms become undeniable
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. That is the cycle the chapter traces, and the teacher who waited three more years is its worked example: the harm compounds because the unexplained struggle doesn't stay unexplained — myths fill the gap, and the person adopts them about themselves. Option C is the common assumption the chapter specifically corrects, since self-stigma is the endpoint. Options B and D contradict the compounding harm the chapter describes.

#### 10. You encounter a confident claim about an ADHD treatment on social media. Which move best reflects the chapter's approach to evaluating ADHD information?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Accept it if the person describing it has ADHD themselves, since lived experience settles the question
2. Reject it automatically, since no social media content about ADHD is reliable
3. Accept it if several accounts you follow repeat the same claim
4. Check it against sources built for the purpose — consensus statements and evidence reviews — rather than judging by confidence or popularity
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The chapter's position is that knowing the definition's shape and checking claims against evidence-based sources inoculates readers against both casual dismissals ("everyone's a little ADHD") and casual self-diagnosis. Options A and C substitute authority or repetition for evidence, and repetition across accounts is how misinformation spreads rather than how it is verified. Option B is too blunt — the chapter teaches filtering, not blanket rejection.

    **Concept Tested:** Evidence-Based Information
